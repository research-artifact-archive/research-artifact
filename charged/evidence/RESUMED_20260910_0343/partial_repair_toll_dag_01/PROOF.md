# Proof obligations before the first grid execution

Let W_i be reachable maximum dirty work, sigma_i the minimum writes attaining it, H(B)=max_{sum b_i<=B} sum G_i(b_i), and M=n*kappa+sum W_i. Assume kappa>0, fresh preparation for every attempt, fixed repeatable footprints, persistent completions, no cross-job write and universal n+r call cap. Let K(B) be the supplied-budget minimum worst total repair-plus-call cost among programs respecting the cap. An aware program may be restricted to its supplied budget for the work guarantee; it can always be made universally call-bounded by forced acceptance on the last allowed call.

Sufficiency for every DAG: if kappa>=max_i W_i, always accepting has value n*kappa+H(B). Fix a maximizing allocation; on the first attempted observation for each job present its allocated set, and after any rejection keep that job clean. If a job accepts its first set, it pays allocated damage. Otherwise it incurs an extra call toll at least as large as that allocated damage. Total cost is at least n*kappa+H(B); inter-job selection does not change this argument.

Necessity for unrestricted ordering, r>=1: any simultaneous optimum cannot reject after a prefix whose completed jobs were all fully dirty. If it did, saturating every remaining attempt until completion would force cost >M at some finite budget, whereas first acceptance caps cost by M. Thus a fully dirty first-observation environment forces it to accept all jobs and pay M within S=sum sigma_i writes. If W_j>kappa and j can be placed last, an aware comparator at B=S accepts preceding jobs. If any observation is not fully dirty it accepts everything; total cost is strictly below M, since the outcome set is finite. If every preceding job and j are fully dirty, at least S writes are already spent: reject j once, then complete cleanly at cost M-W_j+kappa<M. Such j can be put last exactly when it is a sink of the DAG (or any job when independent). Hence a sink W_j>kappa precludes a common optimum. This proves iff kappa>=max W_i for independent jobs, not for arbitrary DAGs.

Precedence counterexample (already observed): A has weights 1,2 and singleton writes; C has weight 1; A->C, r=1, kappa=2. First acceptance gives total curve 4,6,7,8 for B=0,1,2,>=3. Matching lower bounds after subtracting mandatory toll 4: at B=1 first dirty A's weight-2 component, so acceptance or rejection costs at least 2; at B=2 do the same, then dirty C on acceptance (cost3) or A's weight-2 component after rejection (extra toll2+damage2>=4); at B=3 first saturate A with two writes, then dirty C on acceptance (4) or A's weight-2 component after rejection (extra2+damage2=4). These strategies cannot be evaded by another rejection (r=1). Monotonicity and cap prove all larger budgets. Reverse order C->A has expensive sink W_A=3>kappa=2 and therefore no common optimum by the preceding argument. This refutes a DAG-independent criterion kappa>=max W_i; it does not supply a complete arbitrary-DAG criterion.

Finite testing ceiling: saturating every attempted observation forces total accepted work sum W_i. An admissible run uses at most n+r calls and at most S+r max sigma_i writes. Any attempted (r+1)st rejection can be reached within that same bound by stopping before any nonexistent further call; it violates admissibility. Since all-accept caps cost by M, K equals M by this budget. Every observed history's least-consistent write count is at most this ceiling; extra repeated writes cannot yield a smaller comparator. Therefore checking terminal accumulated cost against K(e) for every history characterizes all-budget policy existence in these finite instances. Only the DP/existence search, not a theorem-predicate shortcut, supplies experimental answers.
