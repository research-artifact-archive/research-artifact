\title{Fine-Grained Dynamic Update Controller Synthesis}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}
\renewcommand{\shortauthors}{Anonymous Author(s)}

\begin{abstract}
Verified old and new controllers leave their connecting update unchecked. Components may change at different times, under requirements with different lifetimes. Fine-Grained Dynamic Update Controller Synthesis (FG-DUCS) expresses these choices as a contract between fixed controllers. A finite reachability game and lazy exploration return a policy, progress rank and handover targets, or a losing-region certificate. On \ApplicationContracts{} contracts from \ApplicationModels{} DUCS-derived inputs, lazy exploration decides \LazyDecisions{} within a 1,200~s tool-run limit and 64~GiB Java heap; full-graph exploration in the same implementation decides \DirectFullDecisions{}. Witnesses separate local from simultaneous transfer and lazy from full-graph exploration. Alternative priorities can be faster and one contract is unrealizable. All three on-the-fly variants decide only \TravelOTFDecisions{}/\TravelSettings{} Travel settings, versus \TravelFullDecisions{} for full-graph exploration. Guarantees cover the supplied game; execution and requirement interpretation have separate assumptions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}\label{sec:example}
Updating software while it remains in service must preserve work already in progress~\cite{Cheng2009SASRoadmap,Andersson2013SEAMSProcesses,Giuffrida2013SafeLiveUpdate,Sokolowski2022DynamicWorkflowUpdates}. A production cell may need new station software while a workpiece is in the line; a workflow engine may need new requirements while cases are running. Stopping and emptying the system would interrupt that work. Verifying the old and new controllers separately leaves their connecting update unchecked: components change at different times, requirements begin or expire, and the update can be requested in any state reached during normal operation.

Kubernetes rollouts and MongoDB's Community-to-Enterprise replica-set migration replace replicas in stages, waiting for readiness or rejoining~\cite{e6_kubernetes_deployments,e6_mongodb_enterprise_replica_set}.
These practices motivate contracts that distinguish local replacement, possible outcomes and requirement lifetimes; Section~\ref{sec:rq2} tests constructed abstractions of these mechanisms.

Dynamic Update Controller Synthesis (DUCS) synthesizes update and subsequent control~\cite{Nahabedian2016SEAMS,Nahabedian2020}. Its inputs relate old to new environment states and stop/start whole requirement sets; intermediate stages can be encoded in the new environment. FG-DUCS instead takes local component changes (transfers) and individual requirement lifetimes, and synthesizes their interleaving with normal operations.

Old and chosen new controllers remain fixed. The engineer supplies allowed transfers and outcomes, requirements before/during/after transfer, permitted boundary orders and handover targets. FG-DUCS returns a policy selecting operations from the current state, or evidence that no policy satisfies this contract. Every reachable old state and every possible outcome must finish safely.

Our contributions are this contract and finite reachability game; a terminating, sound and complete lazy procedure with success/failure evidence checkable relative to shared successor and endpoint predicates; and checks and measurements addressing the obstacles below. On \ApplicationContracts{} contracts from \ApplicationModels{} adapted inputs, lazy exploration decides \LazyDecisions{} within budget, versus \DirectFullDecisions{} for full construction; failures and faster alternatives remain reported. Game guarantees concern active monitors and finite completion; requirement interpretation and execution need Section~\ref{sec:interpretation-execution}'s additional conditions.

\section{Motivating Example}
\label{sec:mechanism-boundary}
Consider a production cell with two stations, $A$ and $B$, passing one workpiece between them~\cite{Lewerentz1995ProductionCell,Nahabedian2020}. Both stations receive new versions, with inspection added at $B$. The old requirement (rule) forbids inspection; the new quality requirement requires inspection before the workpiece leaves $B$. The upgrade must continue the work in progress, from either station holding the workpiece.

Each station is replaceable only while empty. Both are never empty together, so replacing everything at once has no safe moment. The policy in Figure~\ref{fig:update-overview} replaces the empty station first and uses ordinary movement to free the other. It shows one way to finish from either starting situation.

The contract requires the new requirement to start before the old one stops, leaving no gap. On the upper path, $B$ holds the workpiece when the new requirement starts: inspection becomes pending. Moving the workpiece out would violate the new requirement; inspecting it would violate the old requirement. Replacing the empty $A$ remains possible, but cannot clear this inspection obligation. The shown policy stops the old requirement, inspects, then finishes replacement. On the lower path, the workpiece leaves $B$ before the new requirement starts, so the policy finishes without inspection. Handover---passing control to the new controller---requires no inspection to be pending.

\input{figures/update_overview.tex}

Inspection leaves the workpiece at $B$ and remains enabled after it has been inspected. Repeating it forever would obey both currently active requirements but never complete replacement. A correct policy must choose a finite route to handover, not just avoid requirement violations. Other finite routes are possible: ordinary movements permit either fixed replacement order.

The engineer provides the verified controllers, both station versions and the conditions below; FG-DUCS returns the missing policy or evidence that none exists. Section~\ref{sec:cell-finite} checks the policy; S1 gives the finite LTSs.
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lp{.73\linewidth}@{}}\toprule
Replacement & Replace a station only when empty.\\
Old requirement & Forbid inspection until this requirement stops.\\
New requirement & Inspect before leaving $B$; start this requirement before stopping the old one.\\
Interval requirement & Keep exactly one workpiece throughout the update.\\
Handover & Finish all changes with no inspection pending and a matching new-controller state.\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Three obstacles organize the rest of the paper. \textbf{L1: local changes may succeed when simultaneous replacement cannot}; Section~\ref{sec:cell-finite} proves this existence result and RQ2 checks granularity witnesses. \textbf{L2: every starting state and outcome must finish safely}; Theorem~\ref{thm:correctness} proves the policy guarantee and RQ1 checks its implementation. \textbf{L3: additional components can multiply the intermediate version combinations}; Section~\ref{thm:lazy-separation} proves an exploration gap on a constructed family, and RQ3 measures savings and limits on the adapted models and scaling families.

The cell's events are all controllable. Adversarial outcomes in L2 appear in Railcab's transfers (Section~\ref{sec:railcab-policy}); the scaling cost in L3 appears in Section~\ref{thm:lazy-separation}'s component family, not in this cell.

\section{Defining an Update Contract}
\label{sec:formalization}

An update contract fixes the component transfers, active requirements and handover conditions. The game makes every permitted event and its outcomes explicit.
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}\toprule
Notation & Meaning\\\midrule
$g_i,\;\rho_i$ & Local transfer relation and its one-shot transfer event\\
$R^{\rm old},R^{\rm new},R^{\rm upd}$ & Old, new and interval requirements\\
$\iota_r,\;Z_{\rm load}$ & Monitor initializer and supplied handover targets\\
$Q_0,\;\Post,\;\Goal$ & All entry states, complete successors and goals\\
$\sigma,\;\rank,\;\kappa$ & Policy, progress rank and handover map\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}


\subsection{Supplied Choices}

\paragraph{Finite LTSs and safety monitors.}
An LTS $\mathcal L=(S,\Sigma_{\mathcal L},\delta,s^0)$ has nondeterministic transitions $\delta:S\times\Sigma_{\mathcal L}\to2^S$. Partition events as $\Sigma=\Sigmauc\uplus\Sigmac$: controllers may disable controllables but cannot select outcomes or suppress uncontrollable (UC) events. A synchronous-product event requires a transition in every component whose alphabet contains it; other components retain their state~\cite{MageeKramer2006}.
A monitor is an automaton that checks a requirement as events occur. Requirement $r$ has a total deterministic monitor $t_r=(M_r,\Sigma,\eta_r,m_r^0,F_r)$ with absorbing errors for violating prefixes~\cite{AlpernSchneider1985,KupfermanVardi2001}. The empty monitor product has one non-error state. Monitors observe labels; policies also observe successor component states.

\label{def:fg-inputs}
\label{def:update_plan}
\paragraph{Components and transfers.}
For each $i\in I$, let $e_i^{\mathrm{old}},e_i^{\mathrm{new}}$ be the pre-update and post-update components and let $g_i\subseteq S_{e_i^{\mathrm{old}}}\times S_{e_i^{\mathrm{new}}}$ be their state-transfer relation.
  Define $g_i(s)=\{s'\mid(s,s')\in g_i\}$. The supplied $g_i$ includes every modeled transformation outcome. Let $E^v=\mathop{\parallel}_{i\in I}e_i^v$.

\paragraph{Requirements and activation.}
Let pairwise-disjoint $R^{\mathrm{old}},R^{\mathrm{new}},R^{\mathrm{upd}}$ contain the pre-update, post-update, and update-interval safety requirements, respectively.
For each $r\in R^{\mathrm{new}}\cup R^{\mathrm{upd}}$, the input supplies a partial \emph{monitor-initialization map} $\iota_r$ from version-tagged component tuples to $M_r\setminus F_r$. Its domain specifies where activation is allowed; in the cell, initialization records whether inspection is pending.
Residual soundness (RS, Section~\ref{sec:monitor-meaning}) relates the installed state's continuations to the declared activation histories. For $r\in R^{\mathrm{upd}}$, $\operatorname{dom}\iota_r$ includes every reachable old component state.

\paragraph{Endpoints and handover targets.}
\label{def:endpoint_controllers}
For $v\in\{\mathrm{old},\mathrm{new}\}$, the supplied finite controller $C^v$ preserves uncontrollables and makes $E^v\parallel C^v$ deadlock-free and satisfy $R^v$. Hiding controller auxiliary events leaves observation alphabet $\bigcup_i\Sigma_{e_i^v}$; the union over both versions is the ordinary alphabet $\SigmaN$. Write $\Reach$ for initial-state reachability and
\[
\mathsf{CL}^v=E^v\parallel C^v\parallel\mathop{\parallel}_{r\in R^v}t_r.
\]
Monitors are passive: every endpoint transition originates in $E^v\parallel C^v$; a monitor only updates on that label and adds no enabled event.
The input supplies nonempty, checked targets $Z_{\mathrm{load}}\subseteq\Reach(\CLnew)$ with component, controller and new-monitor states. FG-DUCS keeps the supplied new controller fixed; the evaluation admits as targets all of $\Reach(\CLnew)$. For a supplied maximally permissive controller this is its reachable winning region, equal to the full region only if all winning states are represented and reachable.

\paragraph{Update commands and order.}
The set of update events, each executed exactly once, is
  \begin{equation}
  \SigmaU=
    \{\rho_i\mid i\in I\}
    \cup\{\ustop{r}\mid r\in R^{\mathrm{old}}\}
    \cup\{\ustart{r}\mid r\in R^{\mathrm{new}}\}.
  \end{equation}
These distinct controllable events transfer a component, stop an old monitor or start a new one, respectively. They are disjoint from ordinary events $\SigmaN$:
$\Sigma=\SigmaN\uplus\SigmaU$ and $\SigmaU\subseteq\Sigmac$. Each transfer event $\rho_i$ is one atomic model transition: the controller chooses the update, but cannot choose its $g_i$ outcome.
They require uncontrollable quiescence (Section~\ref{sec:game-rules}).

An optional strict partial order $\prec\ \subseteq\SigmaU\times\SigmaU$ expresses precedence among update operations. For a pending-event set $P$, event $a$ is order-enabled exactly when $a\in P$ and every $a'\prec a$ has left $P$.
Monitors self-loop on update events that they do not specify.
\paragraph{Eligible contracts.}
Eligibility requires finite, consistently typed states/events and consistent controllability; total deterministic monitors with absorbing errors; well-typed transfers and non-error initializers covering every interval entry; and strict partial-order dependencies. Both endpoints must be safe, deadlock-free and uncontrollable-preserving, with controller alphabets containing every environment label; handover targets must be nonempty and reachable. Separately, the modeler declares admissible activation histories $\mathsf H_r(\xi)$ and justifies RS (Section~\ref{sec:monitor-meaning}). Histories parameterize interpretation, not the game solver; eligibility does not check their completeness. Interval-wide obligations belong to $R^{\mathrm{upd}}$ and post-handover obligations to $R^{\mathrm{new}}$; $\ustart{r^{\mathrm{new}}}\prec\ustop{r^{\mathrm{old}}}$ enforces overlap.

\subsection{States, Transitions, and Completion}
\label{sec:game-rules}

\paragraph{The induced game.}
\label{def:configuration}
\label{def:init}
\emph{States and all entry states.}
An update state $q=(\xi,\mu,P)$ records each component's version/state $\xi(i)=(v,s)$ with $s\in S_{e_i^v}$, each monitor's state $\mu(t_r)\in M_r\cup\{\bot\}$ ($\bot$ means inactive), and pending commands $P\subseteq\SigmaU$.
Here $\rho_i\in P$ exactly when component $i$ is old. Old monitors remain active until their stop leaves $P$; new monitors become active when their start leaves $P$; update monitors are always active. 

Admission $\Init(z)$ inherits $z$'s component and old-monitor states, initializes update monitors through $\iota_r(\xi)$, leaves new monitors inactive and sets $P=\SigmaU$. The policy replaces $C^{\mathrm{old}}$ and discards its internal state and scheduling restrictions. Old monitors remain until their stops; undeclared objectives, including old liveness, are not preserved. The entry set is
\begin{equation}
 Q_0=\{\Init(z)\mid
 z\in\Reach(\mathsf{CL}^{\mathrm{old}})\}.
\label{eq:initial}
\end{equation}
Requiring all reachable old states makes admission independent of waiting for a favorable old state. Selecting an admission predicate would be a different contract. In the cell, $Q_0=\{q_A^0,q_B^0\}$ distinguishes the two holders.
\par\noindent\emph{Four kinds of induced transitions.}
\label{def:successor}
For $\xi(i)=(v,s)$, component $i$ uses $e_i^v$. The active-component product determines enabled ordinary uncontrollables $U(q)$, including those with unsafe outcomes, independently of updates and goals. For every $a\in\SigmaU$, impose
\begin{equation}
 U(q)\ne\emptyset\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Post(q,a)=\emptyset.
 \label{eq:nonpreemption}
\end{equation}
Here, \emph{uncontrollable quiescence} means that no modeled uncontrollable event is enabled. It differs from transaction-based quiescence~\cite{KramerMagee1990Quiescence} and tranquility~\cite{VandewoudeEtAl2007Tranquility}; Section~\ref{sec:runtime-boundary} states the additional execution assumptions. Global quiescence is an explicit modeling restriction, not a consequence of local state transfer. This precondition restricts update events only. Ordinary controllables may still coexist with uncontrollables, although suppressing them preserves winning decisions (Lemma~\ref{lem:uc-priority}). $\Post(q,a)$ contains exactly the successors below; unmentioned coordinates remain unchanged. ``Advance'' applies $\mu'(t_r)=\eta_r(\mu(t_r),a)$ to the specified active monitors.

\begin{description}
  \item[Ordinary event $a\in\SigmaN$.]
  If $a$ belongs to the local event set of at least one active component and every active component having $a$ can transition, take every synchronous-product outcome and advance every active monitor.

  \item[Component update $\rho_i$.]
  If $U(q)=\emptyset$, order-enabled, and $\xi(i)=(\mathrm{old},s)$, set $\xi'(i)=(\mathrm{new},s')$ for each $s'\in g_i(s)$, delete $\rho_i$ from $P$, and advance every active monitor.

  \item[Requirement deactivation $\ustop{r}$.]
  If $U(q)=\emptyset$ and order-enabled, advance every active monitor except $t_r$, retain inactive monitors at $\bot$, set $\mu'(t_r)=\bot$, and delete $\ustop{r}$ from $P$; $t_r$ does not observe it.

  \item[Requirement activation $\ustart{r}$.]
  If $U(q)=\emptyset$, order-enabled, and $\xi\in\operatorname{dom}\iota_r$, advance every active monitor, retain other inactive monitors at $\bot$, install $\mu'(t_r)=\iota_r(\xi)$, and delete $\ustart{r}$ from $P$; $t_r$ begins online observation afterward.
\end{description}
On the upper cell path, $\mathsf{start}$ preserves old/empty $A$ and new/holding $B$, installs inspection-pending in the new monitor and removes itself from $P$. The old and interval monitors stay active.

If a precondition is false, the successor set is empty; every nondeterministic outcome is included and environment-chosen.
Let $\mathcal Q$ contain every state reachable from $Q_0$ through these transitions. 
\par\noindent\emph{Safety and handover-ready goals.}
\label{def:safe_goal}
An update state $q=(\xi,\mu,P)$ is safe, written $\Safe(q)$, exactly when every active monitor is outside its error states.
For $P=\emptyset$ and $z\in Z_{\mathrm{load}}$, write $q\sim z$ when component states (without version tags) and all new-monitor states agree. The handover map $\kappa$ selects such a full endpoint state $z$. Goals are
\begin{equation}
\Goal=\{q=(\xi,\mu,\emptyset)\in\mathcal Q\mid
\Safe(q)\land U(q)=\emptyset\land\exists z\in Z_{\mathrm{load}}:q\sim z\}.
\label{eq:goal}
\end{equation}
At completion, all components and active endpoint monitors are new. Interval obligations end at the goal; new monitors continue after handover and must match. Compatible targets can differ in later behavior while satisfying the endpoint assumptions. At a quiescent goal, handover disables ordinary controllables and installs the controller state in $\kappa(q)$, outside $\Sigma$ and the update rank; no uncontrollable can compete. A matching state with an enabled uncontrollable is not yet a goal.
\par\noindent\emph{State-dependent policies and finite completion.}
\label{def:strategy}
A memoryless update policy is a function
$\sigma:\mathcal Q\setminus\Goal\to2^{\Sigmac}$ selecting only events with nonempty successor sets.

The possible next states under this policy are
\begin{equation}
 \SuccSet_\sigma(q)=
 \bigcup_{u\in U(q)}\Post(q,u)
 \ \cup\
 \bigcup_{a\in\sigma(q)}\Post(q,a).
\label{eq:strategy-successors}
\end{equation}
The environment chooses any uncontrollable and any permitted-event outcome. An uncontrollable self-loop blocks updates by Equation~\eqref{eq:nonpreemption} and permits a losing infinite run. The returned policy retains one controllable at a quiescent state.

Let $\Reach_\sigma(Q_0)$ be the policy closure from $Q_0$, stopping at goals. 

\emph{Runs, winning, and witnesses.}
A run follows the policy successors and stops at a goal. It is \emph{maximal} if infinite or ending at a goal or a state without successors. A single policy $\sigma$ is winning for an FG-DUCS instance if, from every $q_0\in Q_0$,
(i) every reachable update state is safe, (ii) every reachable non-goal state has a successor, and
(iii) every maximal update run reaches $\Goal$ after finitely many events.
This \emph{finite completion} counts observed transitions and assumes no fairness.

A \emph{progress-ranking function} for a winning policy is $\rank:\Reach_\sigma(Q_0)\to\mathbb N$, with value 0 at goal states and satisfying $\rank(q')<\rank(q)$ for every non-goal state $q$ and every
$q'\in \SuccSet_\sigma(q)$.

\paragraph{The FG-DUCS problem.}
The input is an eligible contract: fixed endpoints, paired components and local transfers, safety monitors and initializers, update precedence and handover targets. Decide whether one policy safely completes the induced game from every entry state under every permitted event and outcome. Return $(\sigma,\rank,\kappa)$ covering all $Q_0$, or a losing-region certificate.


\subsection{Checking the Cell}
\label{sec:cell-finite}
S1 tabulates the complete component, controller and monitor LTSs and the policy: ordinary events $m_A,m_B,j$ move to $A$, move to $B$ and inspect at $B$, and all events, including the one-shot transfers $\rho_A,\rho_B$ and requirement boundaries $\mathsf{start}\prec\mathsf{stop}$, are controllable.
With empty-only transfers $g_A=g_B=\{(e,e)\}$ and exactly one workpiece, the two paths $\rho_B;m_B;\mathsf{start};\mathsf{stop};j;\rho_A$ from $A$ holding and $\rho_A;m_A;\mathsf{start};\mathsf{stop};\rho_B$ from $B$ holding have safe singleton outcomes: start installs inspection-pending if $B$ holds and inspection-clear otherwise, stop removes the inspection ban, and both paths finish with new versions and clear inspection at matching supplied targets.
Their remaining lengths $6,\ldots,0$ and $5,\ldots,0$ strictly decrease, proving finite completion from both reachable old states (13 policy states and 11 edges).
Replacing the two transfer commands by one with relation $g_A\times g_B$, with endpoints and requirements fixed, requires both stations empty, but every ordinary move, inspection and requirement boundary preserves exactly one workpiece; that command can never execute, stays pending and makes every goal unreachable.

\section{Certifying On-the-Fly Synthesis}
Lazy (FG-DUCS-OTF) avoids constructing the entire reachable game, while retaining every outcome of each queried event and every uncontrollable choice. It combines finite reachability reasoning with on-the-fly product exploration~\cite{Cimatti1998StrongPlanning,TripakisAltisen1999,CiolekEtAl2023OTFDCS}.
\label{sec:algorithm}

A \emph{bucket} contains every outcome in $\Post(q,a)$; emptiness means the event is disabled. With uncontrollables enabled, every UC outcome must win. Otherwise, one nonempty controllable bucket suffices if all its outcomes win. Lazy generation postpones other choices; Lemma~\ref{lem:uc-priority} permits omitting controllable queries at UC-enabled states.


\subsection{Lazy Successor Generation}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:otf} builds a reachability attractor on the fly~\cite{CassezEtAl2005TimedGames}. It tracks discovered states $V$, states with all UC candidates queried $X\subseteq V$, and proved winners $W$. States in $X$ may retain unqueried controls; $W$ need not contain all winners.

\paragraph{Discover.}
Queries add every outcome to $V$. Goals enter $W$ at rank zero; safe non-goals enter $\mathsf{Open}$, served before retries.

\paragraph{Expand or retry.}
Expansion queries all UC candidates first. If none is enabled, it queries controls through the next nonempty bucket or exhaustion, retaining an unfinished cursor in $\mathsf{Retry}$. Candidates include local events and order-enabled pending commands, covering every enabled event; any fixed finite order is correct.

\paragraph{Promote.}
An expanded safe state's \emph{proving set} is the complete UC-successor union if UC is enabled, or one nonempty control bucket at quiescence. When this nonempty set lies in $W$, the state wins with fixed rank one above its maximum successor rank. Reverse incidences notify predecessors of new winners and trigger reconsideration of recorded buckets. Failure requires exhausted candidates and a promotion fixed point.

On the cell's $A$-holding path, promotion works backward from the rank-zero goal. Inspection self-loops provide no lower-rank successor; nondeterministic buckets require every outcome to win.

Once all entries win, extraction retains one lower-rank control bucket at each reached quiescent non-goal and no controls elsewhere. Its choice can differ from promotion and need not yield shortest paths.

\label{alg:otf}

\begin{lemma}[UC priority]
\label{lem:uc-priority}
Disabling controllable events at UC-enabled states preserves the existence of a policy for finite safe completion.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A winning policy must handle every UC outcome. Disabling optional controls removes runs and creates no deadlock while a UC event remains enabled. Every surviving maximal run was already a maximal run of that policy, so it still completes safely. Conversely, the reduced policy is admissible in the original game because controllers may disable those events.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Search coverage]
\label{lem:search-coverage}
At loop boundaries, every state in $X$ has all UC candidates queried; every expanded unproved quiescent safe non-goal with candidates left is in $\mathsf{Retry}$; and every discovered safe non-goal outside $X$ is in $\mathsf{Open}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Initialization at entry states establishes coverage. Discovery queues each new safe non-goal; only expansion removes it, after querying all UC candidates. At quiescence, expansion and retries consume the cursor through a nonempty bucket or exhaustion, retaining every nonwinner with candidates left. Promotion removes only winners. These are the only changes to those sets; finite propagation drains before either return.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Correctness and Certificates}
The state records versions, monitor memory and pending commands.

\paragraph{Lemma (memoryless policies suffice).}
If any history-dependent policy wins this finite game, a state-based policy wins.
\emph{Proof.} Starting with goals, attractor layers add safe states whose nonempty proving sets lie in previous layers. Choosing those sets gives a state-based policy with decreasing layer index. Outside the fixed point, the environment can keep an outcome outside after every history, or force an unsafe state or deadlock, defeating any history-dependent policy (S2).

\paragraph{Lemma (monitor intervals).}
Along every update prefix, each active monitor is its inherited or initialized state advanced over exactly the labels in its declared active interval.
\emph{Proof.} Entry inherits old states and initializes interval monitors. Ordinary events and transfers advance all active monitors once. Stop removes its monitor before observation, while others advance. Start advances existing monitors and installs the new one after its boundary. Pending commands enforce one-shot execution and consistent versions/activity. Induction gives the stated interval; safety makes it non-error, and goal matching preserves new-monitor states into the endpoint.




\begin{theorem}[Termination, soundness, and completeness]
\label{thm:correctness}
\label{thm:termination}
\label{thm:soundness}
\label{thm:completeness}
For every eligible finite FG-DUCS instance with Equation~\eqref{eq:nonpreemption} and quiescent goals in Equation~\eqref{eq:goal}, Algorithm~\ref{alg:otf} terminates. A returned $(\sigma,\rank,\kappa)$ keeps every active monitor non-error, preserves every uncontrollable event and every outcome of each policy event, reaches $\Goal$ finitely without deadlock, and gives each reached goal a compatible target in $Z_{\mathrm{load}}$ for the supplied game. It returns no solution iff no winning policy covers $Q_0$ in that supplied game.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Termination.} The finite input induces finitely many states and candidates. Each state is discovered and expanded at most once; each retry consumes a new candidate, and each promotion adds a state to $W$ once. Reverse incidences are processed on those promotions, so every worklist drain is finite. Eventually all entries win or both exploration queues empty.

\emph{Soundness.} Induction on promotions gives complete successor sets with smaller retained ranks: expansion supplies all uncontrollables, or the selected nonempty controllable bucket supplies all outcomes. A queried bucket never changes, and ranks are fixed on first promotion, so this evidence persists. If all entries win, their policy closure is safe and every non-goal has a successor. Strict decrease of the natural-number rank excludes infinite runs; nonempty successors exclude non-goal sinks. Thus every maximal run reaches a goal within its initial rank many events. Goal recognition uses modeled uncontrollable quiescence and supplies each compatible $\kappa(q)$.

The monitor-interval lemma above supplies the interval invariant; goal matching preserves components and new-monitor states into the verified endpoint.

\emph{Completeness.} At failure, promotion has reached its fixed point. An entry lies in $L=V\setminus W$, and $L$ contains no goal. By Lemma~\ref{lem:search-coverage}, empty $\mathsf{Open}$ ensures every safe non-goal in $L$ was expanded. With uncontrollables, complete expansion and exhausted propagation imply an uncontrollable outcome in $L$; otherwise promotion would apply. Without uncontrollables, empty $\mathsf{Retry}$ gives every enabled controllable bucket, each with an outcome in $L$ for the same reason. Against any policy, the environment keeps choosing these outcomes; permitting no event produces a deadlock. The resulting run reaches an unsafe state, deadlocks outside a goal, or remains in $L$ forever. Each contradicts winning. Hence a winning policy rules out failure; termination then forces success. 

The losing-region strategy defeats history-dependent policies after every history; success supplies a ranked state-based policy. Lemma~\ref{lem:uc-priority} preserves winning in the original supplied game.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Checking the returned evidence.}
For WIN, let $D_\sigma=\Reach_\sigma(Q_0)$. The checker requires exactly $D_\sigma$ as the rank domain, safety throughout it and, at every non-goal, enabled selected controls, nonempty successor sets inside it, and strict rank decrease for every outcome. At goals it checks rank zero, UC quiescence and compatible $\kappa(q)\in Z_{\rm load}$, with $\operatorname{dom}\kappa=D_\sigma\cap\Goal$; omitted policy entries are empty.

For LOSS, $L\subseteq\mathcal Q$ must include an entry state and exclude all goals. Every safe $q\in L$ must have some UC outcome in $L$ when UC is enabled; otherwise every enabled controllable must have an outcome in $L$. Unsafe states and non-goal deadlocks lose. These conditions certify search relative to shared $\Post$ and endpoint predicates~\cite{HofmannEtAl2016MuCertificates}; Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} states the implementation trust boundary.


\subsection{Exploration Cost and Independent Updates}

With indexed predicates, output-sensitive successors and constant-time bookkeeping, work is linear in discovered states, queried candidates and returned outcomes. Each state is discovered, expanded and promoted at most once; each candidate is queried once and each outcome incidence is processed on promotion. Endpoint preprocessing is excluded. Java also sorts transitions and scans entries (cost model in S2). Direct-Full constructs the same terminalized game first. Worst-case exploration reaches the entire game, potentially exponential in component input.

Every discovered state and nonempty bucket belongs to the Direct-Full game. Both constructions share entries, candidates, $\Post$ and terminal predicates. Inductively, a lazy query starts at a shared nonterminal state and returns exactly outcomes that Direct-Full also generates. Neither expands unsafe states or goals; empty queries add no stored bucket. The next family gives a strict exploration gap, without implying a gap against symbolic representations.

\paragraph{An exponential separation family.}
\label{thm:lazy-separation}
For $n\geq1$, take $n$ components, each with one old and one new state and a singleton transfer. Every version and its endpoint controller admit a controllable $tick$ self-loop, making both endpoints deadlock-free. There are no monitors, dependencies or uncontrollables; the unique new endpoint is available for handover. These inputs are eligible. Each update state is the subset of transferred components, and all $2^n$ subsets are reachable; Direct-Full discovers them all.

Under Lazy's default order, pending transfers precede ordinary events in this family, so expansion follows one $n$-step chain. Because $\mathsf{Open}$ precedes $\mathsf{Retry}$, it reaches the sole goal after discovering exactly $n+1$ states. Backward propagation then assigns ranks $0,\ldots,n$ and proves the entry state before any alternative query. The ordinary self-loops are losing choices under finite completion; both methods use the same terminal goal. Thus the separation concerns exploration of one supplied game.

\subsection{Interpreting Requirement Initializers}
\label{sec:monitor-meaning}
A requirement initializer assigns monitor memory when that requirement starts. Residual soundness connects this memory to the intended history before activation. Let $\mathsf{SafePref}_r$ be the prefix-closed language of requirement $r$, and let $\mathsf H_r(\xi)$ contain all admissible activation histories consistent with the observed component state $\xi$. A new requirement's history includes start once; its initialized monitor reads only later events. Residual-sound initialization means
\[
 \underbrace{\{w:\eta_r^*(\iota_r(\xi),w)\notin F_r\}}_{\text{continuations accepted by the initialized monitor}}
 \ \subseteq\
 \underbrace{\bigcap_{h\in\mathsf H_r(\xi)}\{w:hw\in\mathsf{SafePref}_r\}}_{\text{continuations safe after every admitted history}}
 \tag{RS}\label{eq:residual-soundness}
\]
for every $\xi\in\operatorname{dom}\iota_r$. Equality is exact; strict inclusion is conservative and may exclude feasible updates. An empty history set makes the right side universal. Synthesis checks initializer states and domains, but neither infers histories nor establishes their completeness.

\paragraph{Deriving RS in the cell.}
The declared pre-activation observation retains only $\mathsf{start}$, so $\mathsf H_{r_{\rm inspect}}(\xi)=\{\mathsf{start}\}$ for every admissible cell tuple. The reference monitor starts at $c$, unchanged by start; the right-hand side of RS is therefore its non-error continuation language from $c$. If $B$ is empty, initialization at $c$ gives equality. If $B$ holds, initialization at $d$ is conservative: before the first $j$ or $m_B$, $d$ additionally forbids $m_A$; either $j$ or $m_B$ brings the two initial states to the same state. Thus every word safe from $d$ is safe from $c$, while the single event $m_A$ witnesses strict inclusion. This is a proof for the declared observation scope, not recovery of pre-activation history.

A Boolean event-history variable, or \emph{fluent}, records setting/resetting labels. For initially true $p$, a history making $p$ false then true and the empty history end at the same value but differ under $\Box p$. The evaluation distinguishes three history scopes:
\par\noindent\textbf{A (activation-safe), for new requirements (NEW):} restrict $\mathsf H_r(\xi)$ to safe pre-activation histories.
\par\noindent\textbf{E (entry-scoped), for interval requirements (UPD):} start the reference at update entry.
\par\noindent\textbf{H (history-inclusive, counting pre-activation violations):} count pre-activation violations too; this scope is not claimed. Each chosen scope must match the intended requirement (S3); synthesis alone does not establish that interpretation.

\subsection{Conditions for Execution}
\label{sec:proof}
\label{sec:runtime-boundary}

An execution adapter connects the policy to a running system through the following observations and operations.

\paragraph{Linking the phases.}
\label{def:link}
The linked model connects disjoint old/update/new phases by entry $z\mapsto\Init(z)$, policy edges $\SuccSet_\sigma$ and handover $q\mapsto\kappa(q)$. Entry and handover add no game event. 

Let $\alpha$ be a phase-disjoint observation map.

\noindent\textbf{A1 (endpoints):} actual endpoint observations map to same-labeled edges of $\mathsf{CL}^v$ and preserve its component and monitor states.
\par\noindent\textbf{A2 (update observations and progress):} at a non-goal, filtering the complete environment event set disables every controllable event outside $\sigma(q)$ and disables no uncontrollable event; every remaining event/outcome maps into $\Post(q,a)$ and is reported with its successor identity. The resulting set is nonempty and a next event is eventually observed. This progress premise assumes no fairness among event choices.
\par\noindent\textbf{A3 (commands and observation):} each command starts at uncontrollable quiescence, finishes atomically and failure-free in finite time, and reports one modeled transfer or monitor-lifecycle transition with its successor identity. Internal $\tau$ steps, unobservable outcomes and divergence are outside the model.
\par\noindent\textbf{A4 (boundaries):} entry atomically observes the actual reachable old state $z$ and switches to $\Init(z)$; at a quiescent goal, ordinary controllables are disabled and handover installs its selected controller state and switches control. Each boundary is a finite, failure-free linearizable operation preserving the specified component and monitor states~\cite{HerlihyWing1990}.

\begin{theorem}[Conditional trace lifting]
\label{thm:realization}
For a returned WIN certificate $(\sigma,\rank,\kappa)$ of an eligible contract, every execution satisfying A1--A4 maps through $\alpha$ to the linked model. From an observed entry $\alpha(x_0)=\Init(z)\in Q_0$, the update reaches quiescent handover within $\rank(\Init(z))$ observed game events and then hands control to the compatible endpoint. With complete activation histories as in Section~\ref{sec:monitor-meaning} and RS for the supplied initializers, each declared active interval satisfies its requirement/residual interpretation. Agreement with application intent remains an input obligation.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
A1 maps the old prefix into its monitored endpoint, and A4 maps entry into $Q_0$. A2 preserves every UC move, disables controls outside the policy and maps each report to a complete $\Post$ bucket; A3 makes each command one finite observed step. Induction keeps observations in the safe certificate domain with strictly decreasing rank. A2 excludes stalling, so at most the initial rank many events reach a goal. A4 then hands control to its endpoint finitely; A1 maps the new continuation. Theorem~\ref{thm:correctness} preserves each initialized monitor, and RS makes its continuations legal after every admitted activation history.
\end{proof}

This execution guarantee requires an adapter satisfying A1--A4; we have not evaluated such an adapter.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
Shared authorship, AI assistance and semantics permit correlated faults; the independent parser handles restricted FSP. Application decoding/history completeness and A1--A4 remain unvalidated. A permanent UC loop can block update/handover. Completeness is relative to the supplied game; new-controller resynthesis and contract repair are outside scope.

Inherited inputs show no E1 separation; E5/Threads retain null and both-LOSS cases. Operationally motivated witnesses assume finite startup, reporting, recovery and election and establish existence, not prevalence. PC2's joint-transfer invariant and solver TO are distinct evidence. E2 lacks OTF without UC pruning and does not isolate every runtime cost. Legacy has a different objective; original-source comparison retains \LFUnmeasured{} unprocessed conditions, Railcab size disagreement and repetition variation, while capture-profile stops are author instrumentation.

Completed-pair ratios exclude unresolved costs; S4 preserves every cell, interruption and follow-up. Faster Update-first cases, Industry's contract sensitivity and unresolved Travel/PC settings limit the advantage. Sampling may miss memory peaks. Related fixtures/models/generators and repetitions are not independent applications; one machine limits generalization. Applications have no explicit precedence and only Railcab has branching transfers. Direct-Full is an in-house same-game comparator. External objective-preserving, compositional/symbolic and randomized-order comparisons remain unevaluated; the separation applies to Lazy's default order.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\label{sec:expressiveness}
DUCS combines global transfers and requirement-set boundaries to synthesize update and subsequent control~\cite[Defs.~4.1--4.3]{Nahabedian2020}; its new environment can encode intermediate stages~\cite[Sec.~4.2]{Nahabedian2020}. FG-DUCS generates a fixed-endpoint game from local transfers and individual boundaries. DUCS re-encoding effort and equivalence remain unevaluated. O-DUCS adds update priority and phase-separated analysis; evaluation remains future work~\cite{HiranoEtAl2024ODUCS}.

The GR(1) bridge preserves old then new safety under new-environment safety assumptions; new justice guarantees depend on new justice assumptions~\cite{Amram2022GR1DynamicUpdate}. Live Synthesis carries unfinished obligations forward~\cite{FinkbeinerEtAl2022LiveSynthesis}. Before/after/between scopes~\cite{Dwyer1999Patterns} and event-wise residual rewriting~\cite{RosuHavelund2005Rewriting} precede RS, which quantifies over compatible histories.

State attraction completes a task and reaches configuration startup states within bounded transitions under weak invariance~\cite{NooruldeenSchmidt2015}. Dynamic-feature~\cite{ThuijsmanReniers2024DynamicFeature} and compositional synthesis~\cite{MohajeraniMalikFabian2014Compositional} produce maximally permissive, controllable, nonblocking supervisors for one specification; modular control decomposes its restrictions~\cite{WonhamRamadge1988Modular}. Nonblockingness requires a completing continuation from every reachable state. FG-DUCS connects fixed endpoints and requires every maximal update run to finish.
OTF-DCS requires a marked extension of every controlled finite trace~\cite[Sec.~3]{CiolekEtAl2023OTFDCS}; FG-DUCS requires finite quiescent handover without fairness. Comparing solvers requires an objective-preserving translation.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
FG-DUCS synthesizes model-level policies between fixed controllers from local transfers, requirement lifetimes and handover targets. Lazy exploration saves construction subject to order, contract and resource limits. Execution adapters, relaxed quiescence and same-contract symbolic comparisons remain future work.
