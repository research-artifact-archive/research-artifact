\title{What Retry Budgets Can Guarantee: Calls, Protected Work, and Partial Repair}
\author{Anonymous Author(s)}

\begin{document}
\begin{abstract}
A retry limit bounds failed attempts, but fallback can put all remaining computation under protection. We characterize completion interfaces that support one optimal policy for every unknown write budget. For persistent whole-kernel jobs, cached completion meets a universal call cap $Q\le n+r$ and the exact best protected-work frontier for every budget $B$. Without that operation, the same cap forces all work under protection already at $B=r$. Local and shared retry caps yield different exact frontiers. Partial repair changes whether budget knowledge is useful: a workflow admits one optimum for all budgets exactly when every job has a one-write maximum damage. Charging each call changes this boundary again; we derive an exact one-job minimax loss and a workflow certificate where precedence and past observations matter. Java operations realize the whole-kernel bounds under explicit assumptions. Roslyn modifications preserve checked event, snapshot and compiler projections; stronger rebase operations retain merge costs outside the idealized models. Authored arrivals expose count tradeoffs, foreground burst costs and variable background effects. The results identify guarantees and information requirements of interfaces, while the experiments delimit their operational interpretation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

In optimistic update loops, a caller prepares outside protection and validates before publication. Switching to protected execution after finitely many failures bounds failed calls, but can protect all remaining computation. Which bounds on calls and protected work can be met together, and which completion operations are necessary?

Bounded speculation followed by locking is established practice. With elision enabled, glibc's x86 mutex path defaults to at most three transaction starts before ordinary locking; HotSpot exposes \texttt{RTMRetryCount} for its RTM monitor path~\cite{glibcElision,hotspotRTM}. These are motivation for finite attempt budgets, not instances of our theorem: HTM can abort without a write, rolls back partial computation and does not retain an aborted result for our cached completion. We study explicit validate-and-complete interfaces. A caller may cap failures separately for each update, share an allowance across a batch, or require both. These contracts permit different total call counts and protected work. We characterize all three; a global allowance is a specification choice, not a property inferred from those libraries. No latency SLA is assumed.

Consider two dependent transformations with works 8 and 16 and a cap of two validation or completion calls. Every call must complete a transformation. Fresh protected completion performs all 24 units under protection. Cached completion instead prepares outside and, within one completing callback, either reuses the preparation or recomputes after a mismatch. Its worst protected work is 0, 16, or 24 when at most 0, 1, or 2 writes occur (Figure~\ref{fig:modes}). The policy does not receive this bound. The benefit has a cost: at one write its worst total work is 40, compared with 24 for fresh protected completion.

For works $(1,2,3,5)$, a six-call cap allows $r=2$ failures. Cached completion guarantees at most eight protected work units at four writes. With only fresh fallback, every policy satisfying that universal cap can be forced to protect all eleven units at two writes. Thus cached completion is necessary for this pair of resource requirements. The units count kernel work, not milliseconds.

The operation contract is consequential. If an update can repair only dirty components, our exact single-job result makes the value of knowing $B$ depend on the allowed write footprints. If each call itself is charged, even a small positive toll can destroy simultaneous optimality. These converses tell a developer when importing the whole-kernel guarantee is invalid. Roslyn's prepared content-rebase paths supply a concrete example of a stronger operation whose merge cost must be retained.

We make three contributions: exact local/shared call-protection frontiers, fixed-call-cost loss bounds and an exact one-job minimax choice; a footprint-based characterization of when partial repair admits one optimal policy for all budgets, including workflow precedence, per-call costs and policy certificates; and an operational assessment distinguishing Java realizations, Roslyn semantic projections and observed timing. Earlier supplied-price compilation and its full outcomes remain in the artifact. Fallback, online contention control, quantitative synchronization choices and robust dynamic programming are inherited ideas~\cite{kung,attiya2006,alistarh2018,cerny,cernyquality,mannor}. Our comparison concerns the additional resource equations and their operation-specific lower bounds, not the invention of retry or budget-unaware control.

\paragraph{Three design decisions, three different guarantees.}
A library designer can test whether its completion operations meet a call/protection contract, such as the preceding six-call/eight-work example. This is a conditional design requirement, not an observed production SLA. With partial repair, the write footprints determine whether learning interference can improve the guarantee. With charged calls, our one-job minimax result selects immediate completion or continued speculation. Source experiments test preservation obligations and implementation costs.

\section{Completion Interfaces and Resource Contracts}
\label{sec:interface}

Write $\N=\{0,1,\ldots\}$. Jobs $J=\{1,\ldots,n\}$, $n\ge1$, form a finite DAG with predecessor set $\operatorname{Pred}(i)$. Readiness requires all predecessors completed. Completion records an exact immutable output and persists despite later external writes. The objective is one successful foreground transformation per job, not a simultaneous snapshot or transaction. Policies are deterministic and causal; no randomized expected-cost guarantee is claimed.

Each ready job has a pure, opaque, whole kernel receiving its own-input identity and exact completed predecessor outputs. Every execution performs its fixed work $w_i>0$, whether inside or outside protection. Partial/incremental evaluation, preparation before readiness and discharge by external work are excluded in this interface. A preparation is tied to its own-input identity and predecessor tuple. Captured outputs cannot be reconstructed by later reads of the mutable map.

Local states are $\U$ (unprepared), $\C$ (a retained preparation, possibly stale), or $\D$ (completed), initially all $\U$; several ready jobs may retain preparations. An invalidating write changes an own-input identity while preserving captured predecessor outputs. Identities never recur. The adaptive environment may place a fresh own-key write immediately before a comparison, including a comparison within a completing callback. It spends at most $B$ successful external writes; unused writes are allowed. The controller does not observe future writes. Budget knowledge, when permitted for a comparator, is stated explicitly.

Let $W$ count all foreground kernel work, $L$ its protected part, and $Q$ validation, publication and completion calls, including failures. Preparations, initialization and external-writer calls are excluded from $Q$. These are separate resources. Common call overhead is excluded from $L$. Proposition~\ref{prop:fulltoll} charges it explicitly in this interface; Section~\ref{sec:toll} treats the distinct repair interface. Let $\Omega_k(T)$ sum the largest $\min(k,|T|)$ works in $T$. Write $\Omega(T)$ for the total; omitted sets mean $J$. Set $\Omega_0=0$.

Call the first two validating operations \emph{cheap}: they publish on a match and may return without completion on a mismatch. Their label concerns kernel work; it does not assert a zero-time API call. Two modes provide cheap publication and fresh completion; three also provide cached completion. A failed validation leaves the live entry unchanged. Discarding a preparation is free. An inspection is an unprotected read of the current snapshot or identity; it may retain or discard a preparation, but neither publishes nor holds a guard. These reads and local bookkeeping are outside $Q$. The environment may leave the snapshot unchanged during inspection and write immediately before the next counted comparison, so inspection cannot certify the absence of future interference. Programs can select any ready job after a call, retain preparations and repeat inspections. There is no bare acquire/release, guard-retaining return, cross-job callback or outside completion. All calls and kernels terminate, and foreground scheduling eventually occurs. Noncompletion, including infinite free cycles, has infinite objective. Finite external writing alone does not provide scheduling fairness.

\section{Universal Call and Protected-Work Guarantees}
\label{sec:resources}

For unfinished jobs $S$, let $\mathcal A(S)=\{i\in S:\operatorname{Pred}(i)\cap S=\varnothing\}$ denote the ready jobs.

\begin{theorem}[Protected work under a call cap]
\label{thm:resources}
In Section~\ref{sec:interface}'s interface, fix integers $B,r\ge0$. Among programs supplied with $B$ that complete all jobs with $Q\le n+r$ in every environment with at most $B$ writes, the minimum worst protected work is
\[
 L_3^*(B,r)=\Omega_{\min((B-r)_+,n)},\qquad
 L_2^*(B,r)=\begin{cases}0,&B\le r,\\\Omega,&B>r,\end{cases}
\]
with and without cached completion, respectively. Any DAG is permitted.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
At most $r$ calls may fail to complete. Fresh attempts have disjoint intervals between read and comparison; each failure needs a distinct write. If $B\le r$ they give $Q\le n+B$, $L=0$. Otherwise switch after $r$ failures to cached completion: at most $B-r$ writes remain, giving the stated bound. All-protected gives the two-mode upper bound.

For the two-mode lower bound when $B>r$, reject each cheap call with a fresh own-key write, stopping writes after the $(r+1)$st rejection. This uses at most $r+1\le B$ writes. An admissible program cannot incur that many noncompleting calls, so all its jobs complete under protection.

For three modes, let $k=\min(B-r,n)>0$ and target those $k$ heaviest jobs. Whenever the program compares a target preparation, place a fresh own-key write immediately before that comparison. Cheap calls then fail, and cached calls complete with that target's full work protected. Fresh completion already protects the target, without needing a write. Non-target jobs need no writes.

Here is why the adversary stays within $B$. Let $f$ count target comparisons that did not complete, and $p$ target jobs already protected. Each adversarial write either increases $f$ or completes a cached target; hence the number of writes is at most $f+p$. Before another target write, $f\le r$ and $p\le k-1$, so the next write still gives at most $r+k\le B$. The adversary stops if $r+1$ noncompleting target calls occur. That event would violate the program's cap, since completing all $n$ jobs still needs $n$ other calls. Thus an admissible program protects every target and pays at least $\Omega_k$.

Every chosen write uses a new identity, invalidating all retained target preparations. An inspection or completed callback holds no guard across the next comparison. All schedules used above stop writing after a finite bound; the completion requirement rules out avoiding a target forever.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[One policy for every writer budget]
\label{thm:universal}
Let $\mathcal U_j(r)$ contain the $j$-mode programs that receive no $B$ and complete with $Q\le n+r$ in every finite-write environment. Set $U_j(B,r)=\inf_{P\in\mathcal U_j(r)}\sup_{\#\mathrm{writes}\le B}L(P,E)$. Then
\[
 U_3(B,r)=L_3^*(B,r),\qquad U_2(B,r)=\Omega\,\mathbf1[B\ge r].
\]
One policy per mode family attains these values simultaneously for all $B$ and positive works on any DAG, using only readiness and a global cheap-failure counter.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Switch after $r$ cheap failures to a fresh read/preparation with cached completion (three) or to fresh protected completion (two). This uses at most $r$ extra calls universally. At $B<r$ no protection is needed; at $B=r$ cached completions after switching still match. Theorem~\ref{thm:resources} supplies the remaining upper and three-mode lower bounds.

For two modes when $B\ge r$, reject every cheap call with a fresh own-key write until $r$ rejections, then stop. Only protected calls complete before this. Afterwards, another rejectable call could fail in a different finite environment, violating the call cap. Thus all jobs complete under protection with at most $r\le B$ writes. That additional hypothetical write is absent from the lower-bound execution. Retained preparations and inspections cannot prevent a fresh write before comparison.
\end{proof}

For equal works and $B>r$, the ratio is $n/\min(B-r,n)$, reaching $n$ at $r=B-1$. At $B=r$, an informed two-mode program can take one more cheap call because it knows no write remains. An unaware program must also be safe in extensions containing another write. This difference in the quantified environments is the source of the boundary shift.

The frontier is independent of the DAG edges. Readiness constrains when a target is attempted, but persistent completion requires every target eventually; the adversary does not need to change their legal order. Precedence is therefore harmless for this frontier. Section~\ref{sec:tolldag} shows that it becomes consequential under a different completion interface and call costs.

\subsection{Local Caps and a Shared Cap Can Be Required Together}

Suppose job $i$ may incur at most $r_i$ noncompleting calls, and the batch at most $s\le R=\sum_i r_i$. These caps hold in every finite-write environment. Define the weighted subset value
\[
 A_{\boldsymbol c}(B)=\max_{T\subseteq J:\,\sum_{i\in T}c_i\le B}\sum_{i\in T}w_i,
\]
allowing zero-cost items. This is a knapsack value; the contribution is identifying the write cost that activates each job's protection.
\begin{proposition}[Local and shared resource contracts]
\label{prop:localcaps}
The minimum worst protected work under both caps is
\[
\begin{aligned}
 U_2(B;\boldsymbol r,s)&=\max\{A_{\boldsymbol r}(B),\Omega\,\mathbf1[B\ge s]\},\\
 U_3(B;\boldsymbol r,s)&=\max\{A_{\boldsymbol r+\boldsymbol1}(B),\Omega_{(B-s)_+}\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
One policy attains each curve simultaneously for every $B$: switch to guaranteed completion when either the current local allowance or the shared allowance is exhausted. Its worst call count is $n+\min(B,s)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For a local lower bound, target a maximizing subset. Reject each target's cheap calls up to its local cap. In two modes another rejectable call is then universally inadmissible, so the target completes protectively using at most $r_i$ actual writes. In three modes, one additional write invalidates its eventual cached comparison; an earlier fresh completion already pays $w_i$. Thus each target costs at most $r_i+1$ writes. The shared lower bounds follow from Theorem~\ref{thm:universal}, since adding local constraints cannot enlarge its policy class.

For the switching policy, if fewer than $s$ failures occur, a protected job has exhausted its own cap: it requires $r_i$ writes in two modes, and $r_i+1$ in three. If $s$ failures occur, two modes pay at most $\Omega$; in three, every protected completion still needs its own additional mismatch write, so at most $(B-s)_+$ jobs are protected. These two cases give the maximum above. A fresh preparation after every rejection makes the intervals disjoint. Distributing $\min(B,s)$ cheap failures among the local allowances attains the call bound; its maximum need not coincide with the protection maximum.
\end{proof}

With only local caps take $s=R$, giving $U_2=A_{\boldsymbol r}(B)$ and $U_3=A_{\boldsymbol r+\boldsymbol1}(B)$. Uniform $r_i=r\ge1$ yields $\Omega_{\lfloor B/r\rfloor}$ and $\Omega_{\lfloor B/(r+1)\rfloor}$, with $Q\le n+nr$. At $r=0$ these become $\Omega$ and $\Omega_B$, with $Q=n$. Reusing the rejected preparation at the threshold would lose the extra-write requirement of the three-mode bound. A per-job preparation cap is therefore a different contract. Sharing the same total allowance $R$ permits weakly less worst protected work than reserving local allowances; requiring local limits restricts that flexibility.

\subsection{Choosing Speculation When Every Call Has a Cost}

Let each counted call cost $\kappa\ge0$ and minimize $C=L+\kappa Q$. Let $C^*(B,r)$ be the minimum worst cost over the full $B$-informed program class of Theorem~\ref{thm:resources}, with only the shared cap. Mode choice remains \emph{before} comparison; the partial-repair interface below allows a different observation/action order. Write $C(P,B)$ for policy $P$'s worst cost at budget $B$.

\begin{proposition}[Loss of the global-threshold policy]
\label{prop:fulltoll}
The three-mode policy $P_r$ that freshly prepares before each attempt and switches after $r$ cheap failures to cached completion has
\[
\begin{aligned}
 C(P_r,B)&=n\kappa+\kappa\min(B,r)+\Omega_{(B-r)_+},\\
 0\le C(P_r,B)-C^*(B,r)&\le\kappa\min(B,r)\le r\kappa.
\end{aligned}
\]
The $r\kappa$ bound is sharp for this policy.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The call and protection upper bounds follow from Theorem~\ref{thm:universal}. They are attained together: reject the first $\min(B,r)$ cheap calls, then use remaining writes to invalidate cached completions of the heaviest jobs. Every competitor has a resource lower-bound execution with both $L\ge\Omega_{(B-r)_+}$ and $Q\ge n$. Thus $C^*\ge n\kappa+\Omega_{(B-r)_+}$ on that execution. For one job and $B=r+1$, invalidating every comparison forces protected completion. Immediate cached completion is optimal at $\kappa+w$, while $P_r$ pays $(r+1)\kappa+w$.
\end{proof}

The bound compares a specified policy with an informed comparator. A caller may instead want the best possible loss without ever learning $B$. For one job this question has an exact answer.
\begin{proposition}[Exact one-job minimax additive loss]
\label{prop:regret}
For one job with work $w>0$, let $P$ range over all deterministic programs that receive no $B$ and complete with $Q\le r+1$ in every finite-write environment. Then
\[
 \inf_P\sup_{B\ge0}\bigl(C(P,B)-C^*(B,r)\bigr)
 =\min\{(w-\kappa)_+,r\kappa\}.
\]
Always-cached completion attains the first term for $r\ge1$; $P_r$ attains the second. Choose the smaller, without estimating $B$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The informed one-job value is $\kappa+\min(B\kappa,w)$ for $B\le r$, and $\kappa+w$ for $B>r$. For the first case, cheap attempts and immediate cached completion give the two upper bounds. For a lower bound, count all noncompleting calls in $a$. While $a<B$, invalidate each selected usable comparison with at most one fresh write. Actual writes are at most $a$; a cached completion can therefore be invalidated within $a+1\le B$ writes. Fresh completion already pays $w$. If $a=B$ is reached, stop writing: any subsequent completion has at least $B+1$ calls, regardless of its mode. For $B>r$, invalidating through the cap uses at most $r+1\le B$ writes and forces protected completion.

Now consider an unaware program with $r\ge1$ and $0<\kappa<w$. Again invalidate each chosen usable comparison, and let $a\le r$ count all noncompleting calls before its first fresh/cached completion. If fresh, witness budget $B=a$ gives regret at least $a\kappa+w-\min(a\kappa,w)\ge w$. If cached and $a<r$, one more write and $B=a+1$ give regret at least $a\kappa+w-\min((a+1)\kappa,w)\ge w-\kappa$. If cached and $a=r$, use $B=r+1$ to get $r\kappa$. These are upper bounds on actual writes, not assumed equalities. Stop the adversary after at most $r+1$ noncompleting calls; the cap and finite-write completion exclude an infinite avoidance run. Fresh identities invalidate retained preparations after mode selection, so free inspections do not change the argument.

Always-cached has worst regret $(w-\kappa)_+$ for $r\ge1$. Substituting the informed curve above into Proposition~\ref{prop:fulltoll} gives worst regret $r\kappa$ for $P_r$. Nonnegativity covers $r=0$, $\kappa=0$ and $\kappa\ge w$.
\end{proof}

For $0<\kappa<w$, immediate cached completion is the minimax choice when $w\le(r+1)\kappa$; otherwise use all $r$ retries. For example, $w=8,r=2$ chooses retries at $\kappa=1$ (loss 2 versus 7) and immediate cached completion at $\kappa=3$ (loss 5 versus 6). Exact simultaneous optimality fails in this range, yet its best loss is quantified. These are supplied cost units, not calibrated latency. The all-program one-job result does not assert the best unaware loss for an arbitrary workflow.

\section{When Partial Repair Makes the Budget Valuable}
\label{sec:partial}

Whole-kernel recomputation makes the first invalidation of a preparation as damaging as further invalidations of that preparation. Some software can instead reuse unaffected components. We now characterize when that operation change destroys a common optimum. We first define a single-job repair interface, then extend it to workflows. This is not a reduction of every Roslyn merge to component work.

\paragraph{Repair interface and observations.}
One job consists of $m$ components with positive weights $w_c$. A write changes fresh versions on one nonempty footprint from a fixed finite family $\Gamma$. Footprints may overlap and repeat; each occurrence costs one write. Outside protection, preparation reads and computes every component anew. A protected call observes the dirty set $D$, the union of footprints since that preparation. It either accepts, repairs exactly $D$ and publishes the job, or rejects and prepares again outside. An empty observation can complete with no repair. No partial publication, free dirty-set query, retained protection or cheaper completion operation is available. A fresh whole-job completion costs at least repairing any dirty set. Calls and preparations terminate.

The controller sees $D$ and its history, but neither $B$ nor the number of repeated writes within an interval. It must complete in at most $q\ge1$ calls for every finite-write execution. The metric in the next theorem is protected repair work; common validation/publication overhead is excluded, not assumed absent. Let
\[
G(b)=\max_{0\le t\le b,\ \gamma_j\in\Gamma}
       \sum_{c\in\gamma_1\cup\cdots\cup\gamma_t}w_c,
\qquad W^*=w\!\left(\bigcup\Gamma\right).
\]
Then $G(0)=0$, $G$ is nondecreasing and subadditive, and $G(m)=W^*>0$. Unreachable components do not contribute to $W^*$. Singleton footprints give the prefix sums of weights sorted in decreasing order. A general footprint family need not have a cheap profile: computing these unions is part of the input-processing cost.

\begin{theorem}[Exact value and information boundary for repair]
\label{thm:repair}
For a controller supplied with $B$, minimum worst repair work is
\[
K_q(B)=G(\lfloor B/q\rfloor).
\]
Among controllers receiving no $B$ that attain $K_q(B)=0$ for every $B<q$, the least possible worst-work envelope is
\[
T_q(B)=G((B-q+1)_+).
\]
One policy attains this envelope for all $B$. For $q\ge2$, a single controller is optimal for every $B$ if and only if $G(1)=W^*$. For $q=1$, such a controller always exists.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put $k=\lfloor B/q\rfloor$. The aware policy accepts work at most $G(k)$ and otherwise rejects while a call remains. An observation with greater work requires at least $k+1$ writes. Rejecting $q-1$ such observations exhausts all opportunities for a $q$th: $q(k+1)>B$. Thus forced acceptance on the last call still meets $G(k)$. This policy also satisfies the call cap outside its supplied budget, where the work guarantee is not asserted. Conversely, a word of at most $k$ footprints realizes $G(k)$. Repeat it after every preparation. Any accepting call pays $G(k)$, and $q$ repetitions use at most $qk\le B$ writes. Fresh whole-job completion is no cheaper.

For the unaware lower bound, write once in each of the first $q-1$ preparation intervals. After observation $j<q$, the history is compatible with only $j$ writes in total. Accepting positive damage, or fresh completion, would violate that budget's zero optimum. Hence the policy must reject all $q-1$ times. In its final interval spend up to $B-q+1$ writes realizing $G(B-q+1)$. No retry remains. The matching policy rejects its first $q-1$ nonempty observations, then accepts; earlier empty observations complete for zero work.

Every simultaneous optimum meets the preceding zero-work requirements. If $G(1)<W^*$, let $k\ge2$ be the first index with $G(k)>G(1)$, and set $B=q-1+k$. Then $1\le\lfloor B/q\rfloor<k$, so $K_q(B)=G(1)<G(k)=T_q(B)$. Conversely, if $G(1)=W^*$, both expressions are zero for $B<q$ and $W^*$ otherwise. For $q=1$ their arguments coincide.
\end{proof}

This is a condition on the damage a write can cause, not simply the availability of a partial-repair API. If one allowed footprint covers all reachable work, partial repair still admits simultaneous optimality. If writes affect separate components, additional writes can increase the damage and knowledge of $B$ changes the optimal acceptance threshold.

For two unit components, singleton writes and $q=2$, $K_2(B)$ at $B=0,1,2,3$ is $0,0,1,1$. Optimality at $B=1$ forces an unaware policy to reject its first dirty component. Two subsequent writes make both components dirty; its last call costs 2 at $B=3$. The aware policy at that budget accepts one dirty component immediately and retries only two-component damage. Figure~\ref{fig:information} isolates the shared observation behind this separation.

\paragraph{Quantifying the loss without a budget.}
For repair work alone, a finite competitive ratio requires zero work when $K_q(B)=0$. Theorem~\ref{thm:repair} therefore identifies the exact optimal ratio as $\sup_{B\ge q}T_q(B)/K_q(B)$. Subadditivity gives an upper bound $q$: with $k=\lfloor B/q\rfloor$, $B-q+1\le qk$, so $G(B-q+1)\le qG(k)$. It is sharp for sufficiently many unit singleton components at $B=2q-1$, where the ratio is $q$. Thus the loss can grow with the call allowance; it is not a constant-price perturbation of whole-kernel optimality.

\subsection{A Shared Retry Allowance Across a Workflow}
\label{sec:partialdag}

The same boundary extends beyond a single job. Consider $n$ persistent jobs with a DAG, each with its own component/footprint family. A ready job is freshly prepared before each attempted observation; no other preparation is retained. A write affects the currently attempted job, with no cross-job footprint. After a rejection the next ready job may be selected anew. The total call cap is $n+r$, with a shared $r$; acceptance pays that job's dirty-component work and permanently completes it. Write $W_i=G_i(\infty)$, $\sigma_i=\min\{b:G_i(b)=W_i\}$, $\Sigma=\sum_i\sigma_i$, and $W^*=\sum_iW_i$. Let $K(B)$ be the known-budget minimum worst repair work for this interface.

\begin{theorem}[Workflow information boundary]
\label{thm:repairdag}
For $r\ge1$, one policy simultaneously attains $K(B)$ for every $B$ iff $G_i(1)=W_i$ for every job. This holds both for a fixed order and for choices among DAG-ready jobs. Under the condition,
\[
K(B)=\text{sum of the largest }\min((B-r)_+,n)\text{ values }W_i.
\]
For $r=0$, accepting every observation is simultaneously optimal for arbitrary footprints. More generally, the first budget at which $K(B)=W^*$ is
\[
B_{\rm sat}=\Sigma+r\max_i\sigma_i.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If every job has a one-write maximum, reject the first $r$ nonempty observations globally and otherwise accept. Early acceptances are clean; after $r$ rejections, at most $(B-r)_+$ jobs can accept positive damage. To force the matching lower bound, target that many heaviest jobs, writing once before each target observation to cause maximum damage. A target completion costs $W_i$, and at most $r$ rejections add writes, so $r+k\le B$ writes suffice for $k$ targets. An attempted $(r+1)$st rejection would itself violate the call cap within budget; truncate the adversary there. Selection and precedence do not avoid completing the targets.

For necessity, all $K(B)$ with $B\le r$ are zero. A simultaneous optimum must therefore reject when one write is issued before each of its first $r$ observations: after observation $t$, the history is compatible with only $t\le r$ writes in total. No job has yet completed. Now spend $\sigma_i$ writes to saturate each job on its next attempt. No rejection remains, so the policy pays $W^*$ with $B_0=\Sigma+r$ writes. If some $\sigma_j\ge2$, a competing policy reserves all $r$ rejections for fully dirty observations of job $j$ and accepts everything else. Full total work would require $\Sigma+r\sigma_j>B_0$ writes, as every invocation uses a fresh preparation. Its worst cost is therefore strictly below $W^*$; the attainable cost set is finite. This contradiction proves necessity even in a prescribed order.

At $r=0$, every call must complete and acceptance dominates fresh whole-job completion. The value is $\max_{\sum b_i\le B}\sum_iG_i(b_i)$ independently of order. For the final statement, saturating every attempted observation uses at most $\Sigma+r\max_i\sigma_i$ writes on an admissible run and forces full work. Below that budget, reserve all $r$ rejections for full observations of a maximum-$\sigma_i$ job. Full work would require exactly that many writes, so the worst cost is strictly smaller.
\end{proof}

For two jobs, one with two unit singleton components and one with a single unit component, $r=1$ gives $K(B)=0,0,1,2,2,3$ at $B=0,\ldots,5$ in either order. Yet optimality at $B=1$ forces a first rejection; three subsequent writes saturate both jobs and force cost 3 at $B=4$, where $K(4)=2$. The shared allowance does not remove the information gap. The theorem identifies its existence and saturation threshold; it does not give a closed form for every intermediate value in an arbitrary nonsaturating workflow.

\subsection{Charging Every Call Changes the Boundary Again}
\label{sec:toll}

Repair work omits the protected cost of validation itself. To expose this assumption, charge $\kappa\ge0$ on \emph{each} call and a common $\mu\ge0$ once upon completion. Acceptance on call $t$ costs $\mu+t\kappa+w(D)$. Preparation remains outside this metric. Let $M$ be the sorted multiset of $qm$ numbers
\[
\{\,t\kappa+G(k):1\le t\le q,\ 0\le k<m\,\},
\]
with one-based entries $M_j$ and repeated entries retained.

\begin{theorem}[Toll curve and simultaneous-optimality boundary]
\label{thm:toll}
The known-budget optimum is $\mu+C_q(B)$, where
\[
C_q(B)=\begin{cases}
\min\{\kappa+W^*,M_{B+1}\},& B<qm,\\
\kappa+W^*,&B\ge qm.
\end{cases}
\]
For $q\ge2$, simultaneous optimality holds exactly in the following cases: $\kappa=0$ and $G(1)=W^*$, or $\kappa\ge W^*$. It fails for every $0<\kappa<W^*$. The common cost $\mu$ does not change these conditions. At $q=1$, first acceptance is always simultaneously optimal.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Remove $\mu$ and fix $T<\kappa+W^*$. On call $t$, an unacceptable dirty set has $t\kappa+w(D)>T$. Its least write cost is
$a_t(T)=\min\{k:t\kappa+G(k)>T\}$. Such an index exists since $G(m)=W^*$. A sequence of $q$ unacceptable observations is possible exactly when $\sum_ta_t(T)\le B$: each interval can independently realize a minimum-cost witness. Otherwise accepting the first acceptable observation finishes within $q$ calls and cost $T$. Because $G$ is nondecreasing, $a_t(T)$ counts that shifted list's entries at most $T$. Hence $\sum_ta_t(T)$ is the rank of $T$ in $M$, with ties. The smallest achievable threshold below the first-accept cap is $M_{B+1}$, proving the curve.

If $\kappa>0$ and a policy ever rejects a first observation, follow it by maximum damage until it accepts. At a sufficiently large finite budget it pays at least $2\kappa+W^*$, exceeding the aware first-accept cap $\kappa+W^*$. Thus a simultaneous optimum must accept every first observation. Let $k^*$ be the first index with $G(k^*)=W^*$. With known budget $k^*$, accept all first observations except maximum damage; reject that one and the next observation is necessarily clean. Its worst cost is at most $\max\{\kappa+W_{<},2\kappa\}$, where $W_{<}<W^*$ is the largest attainable nonmaximum damage. For $0<\kappa<W^*$ this is strictly smaller than the first-accept value $\kappa+W^*$. Conversely, when $\kappa\ge W^*$, presenting a first observation with damage $G(B)$ forces either $\kappa+G(B)$ on acceptance or at least $2\kappa$ on rejection. First acceptance attains the former, which is no larger. The zero-toll case is Theorem~\ref{thm:repair}.
\end{proof}

The zero-toll guarantee is therefore sensitive to the objective: even an arbitrarily small positive per-call charge precludes a common optimum until that charge reaches the maximum repair work. This does not refute a bound that deliberately measures kernel work alone. It identifies which overhead can be ignored for that particular optimality claim.

The supplement also compiles the best deterministic competitive acceptance threshold. If $c(D)$ is the least footprint count realizing $D$ and $e$ sums those counts after rejections, acceptance on call $t$ is safe for ratio $R$ iff
$\mu+t\kappa+w(D)\le R(\mu+C_q(e+c(D)))$.
The comparator is smallest at this least-consistent budget. Monotonicity reduces ratio feasibility to a minimum-write failing path, with exact rational upper/lower certificates. Given correct profiles, the specialized compiler uses $O(qm\log(q+1)+qm\log U)$ arithmetic operations and $O(qm+q)$ space, where $U=\mu+q\kappa+W^*$ after integer scaling. Arbitrary footprint profiling and runtime minimum-cover computation remain separate costs; singleton footprints use sorting and $c(D)=|D|$.

\subsection{With Call Charges, Precedence and History Matter}
\label{sec:tolldag}

Return to the workflow interface of Section~\ref{sec:partialdag}, charging $\kappa>0$ per call. Write $K^{\kappa}(B)$ for its known-budget value, $H(B)=\max_{\sum b_i\le B}\sum_iG_i(b_i)$ and $C_{\max}=n\kappa+\sum_iW_i$.

\begin{theorem}[Call charges in workflows]
\label{thm:tolldag}
For independent jobs and $r\ge1$, a simultaneous optimum exists iff $\kappa\ge\max_iW_i$. This condition is sufficient on every DAG: first acceptance has exact value $n\kappa+H(B)$. On a general DAG, a sink with $W_i>\kappa$ prevents simultaneous optimality. The condition on all jobs is no longer necessary.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For sufficiency, fix an allocation attaining $H(B)$. Present its dirty set on each job's first attempt and leave that job clean after a rejection. Accepting pays the allocated damage; rejecting incurs an extra toll at least as large. This matches the first-accept upper bound, regardless of order.

For necessity, a simultaneous optimum cannot reject after a prefix whose completed jobs were all fully dirty: saturating every remaining attempt would then force cost above $C_{\max}$ at some finite budget. Hence fully dirty first observations force it to pay $C_{\max}$ with $\Sigma$ writes. If a job $j$ with $W_j>\kappa$ can be placed last, an aware policy at budget $\Sigma$ accepts preceding jobs. If any observation is less than full it accepts throughout, costing strictly below $C_{\max}$. Otherwise all $\Sigma$ necessary writes are spent; rejecting $j$ once then completing cleanly costs $C_{\max}-W_j+\kappa<C_{\max}$. The finite outcome set makes the worst cost strictly smaller. Every independent job, and every DAG sink, can be last.

For the final claim, let $A$ have singleton components of weights 1 and 2, let $C$ have one component of weight 1, require $A$ before $C$, and take $r=1,\kappa=2$. First acceptance has curve $4,6,7,8$ at $B=0,1,2,\ge3$. Matching lower bounds subtract the mandatory toll 4. At $B=1$, dirty $A$'s weight-2 component: acceptance or rejection costs at least 2. At $B=2$, do the same, then dirty $C$ after acceptance (cost 3), or the weight-2 component again after rejection (cost 4). At $B=3$, first saturate $A$ with two writes; then dirty $C$ after acceptance (cost 4), or the weight-2 component after rejection (cost 4). Thus first acceptance is a common optimum although $\kappa<W_A$. Reversing the order makes $A$ a sink and removes every common optimum.
\end{proof}

History-dependent rejection can be essential even with one-component jobs. Consider chain $(a,h,a)$, $h>a>0$, with $r=1,\kappa=a$. Accept throughout except that a dirty middle job is rejected once if the first job completed cleanly. Its exact curve is $3a,4a,4a+h,5a+h$ at $B=0,1,2,\ge3$. First acceptance instead costs $3a+h>4a$ at one write. For upper bounds, a retry leaves at most $B-1$ writes; whenever its extra toll $a$ is paid, the clean first job has saved $a$. For matching lower bounds, one dirty observation forces at least $a$ beyond baseline. With two writes, leave the first job clean and dirty the middle, then dirty the last after acceptance or the middle again after rejection, forcing $a+h$. With three writes, dirty the first job: after acceptance apply that two-write strategy to the last two jobs; after rejection dirty the middle and last instead. This forces $h+2a$. A preceding clean rejection already spends the toll and leaves no retry. No writer budget is supplied; $(a,h)=(1,3)$ gives the saved seven-case separation.

\paragraph{Constructing a Common Policy for Other DAGs.}
A finite recurrence decides whether a common optimum exists beyond these closed-form cases. Set $E=\Sigma+r\max_i\sigma_i$; saturating every attempt proves $K^{\kappa}(b)=C_{\max}$ for $b\ge E$. Let $e$ be the least-consistent write count clipped to $E$, $t$ the remaining rejections, and $a$ the cost already paid. Define $\Theta(\varnothing,t,e)=K^{\kappa}(e)$ and, with $e_D=\min(e+c(D),E)$,
\begin{equation}
\Theta(S,t,e)=\max_{i\in\mathcal A(S)}\min_D
\max\left\{\Theta(S-i,t,e_D)-\kappa-w_i(D),\
\Theta(S,t-1,e_D)-\kappa\right\},
\label{eq:viability}
\end{equation}
omitting the rejection term at $t=0$. A continuation exists exactly when $a\le\Theta(S,t,e)$; in particular, $\Theta(J,r,0)\ge0$ decides common optimality.

To prove this, consider a completed history and sum the minimum footprint-word lengths of its observations. Fresh preparations and independent own-job writes realize those words in separate intervals, so the sum is the least budget consistent with that history. Every larger budget permits the same history by leaving writes unused. Since $K^\kappa$ is nondecreasing, meeting its value at the least budget is necessary and sufficient for that history to meet every consistent target. Above $E$ all targets equal $C_{\max}$; clipping this coordinate is exact even though a partial observation's minimum word can exceed its job's full-saturation count. Accumulated cost $a$ is never clipped.

Induct on $|S|+t$. With no jobs left, feasibility is the interval $a\le K^\kappa(e)$. For a fixed ready job and observation, accepting adds $\kappa+w_i(D)$ and rejecting adds $\kappa$; either strictly decreases the induction measure. Each child's feasible costs therefore give a shifted downward interval. The choice after observing $D$ takes the larger endpoint. Requiring success for every observation takes the minimum; choosing a job before that observation takes the maximum. This proves Equation~\eqref{eq:viability} with its required $\exists i\,\forall D\,\exists\mathrm{action}$ order. A maximizing job and any satisfied child inequality supply an executable policy using history alone. If the initial interval excludes zero, the failed alternatives identify an adversarial finite observation path for each attempted controller choice.


For each minimum footprint count, only its maximum attainable damage is needed in the adversarial alternatives. With $I$ unfinished sets and $h=\max_i(m_i+1)$, the table has $O(I(r+1)(E+1))$ entries and uses $O(In(r+1)(E+1)h)$ arithmetic operations, given profiles and the comparator. For a fixed order, $I=n+1$ and there is one job choice. This removes accumulated-cost enumeration, not potentially exponential DAG states or footprint profiling. It is a specialization of finite minimax and information-state reasoning, not a new general dynamic-programming principle.

\begin{theorem}[Resource bounds for actual writes]
\label{thm:writes}
Under the stated API, monitor and scheduling assumptions, Theorems~\ref{thm:resources}--\ref{thm:universal}'s exact call/protected-kernel-work bounds hold for Java programs using the listed operations, with separate or shared internal bins. This is a mathematical refinement argument for those operations, not a mechanically verified property of an entire JDK binary.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For the upper bounds, the serial policies freshly read and prepare after every cheap failure. Their read-to-comparison intervals, including cached completions, are disjoint; every mismatch contains a distinct successful write. Thus at most $B$ mismatches occur. Every fresh/cached completing call completes exactly one job, and a cached mismatch performs precisely that job's kernel inside.

For the lower bounds, use the target-job adversaries of Section~\ref{sec:resources}. Immediately before a chosen comparison acquires its monitor, a successful own-key write installs a fresh identity, invalidating every retained preparation for that job. The writer need not run inside the monitor: moving this write before acquisition preserves the selected operation and its mismatch outcome. A cheap call fails; a cached call recomputes inside; a fresh callback already incurs its whole kernel inside. Stale copies cannot complete cheaply, captured parent outputs persist, and no monitor survives the preceding call. Bin collisions therefore do not prevent the next chosen write. The prohibition on other-job work within a callback ensures it cannot complete several targets for one call. The same write counts and hypothetical extra-write argument apply.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Operational accounting.}
Native kernels fold captured predecessor seeds and an immutable array of length $8w_i-|\operatorname{Pred}(i)|>0$, executing exactly $8w_i$ selected operations. Counters record their protected subset and the specified calls separately. Supplementary scalar tests add $8p_i$ per protected kernel, $8v_i$ per conditional call and $8k_i$ per callback. Allocation, monitor waiting, initialization, background work and other API instructions are excluded; charges are not inferred from timing.

\section{Related Work and Research Lineage}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Fallback and online contention management.}
Kung and Robinson~\cite{kung}, Section 3.3/p.220, already retain the critical-section semaphore during reexecution after repeated validation failure. Lock-elision retry caps likewise precede our work~\cite{glibcElision,hotspotRTM}. Cached completion here requires an unrolled-back preparation and atomic identity-check/recompute; HTM capacity or explicit aborts need not consume a write~\cite{gccRTM}. These mechanisms motivate the interfaces but do not establish their joint $Q,L$ guarantee.

Attiya et al.~\cite{attiya2006}, Sections 2--3/5, analyze one Greedy contention manager that does not receive the per-job non-conflict failure bound $k$. It is $O(ks)$-competitive for makespan with $s$ resources, with a corresponding deterministic lower bound under time-dependent resource requests. Thus a single policy for an unknown disturbance level and an all-policy lower bound are established. Our allowance counts global external writes, the cap constrains every finite-write execution, and the output is an exact protected-kernel-work curve for persistent jobs. Alistarh et al.~\cite{alistarh2018}, Sections 4--5, optimize whether and when to abort a conflicting transaction with known abort cost and unknown remaining duration. Their original throughput policy has no progress guarantee; Section 7 adds a probabilistic one. That objective and returned guarantee differ from the universal call cap here.

Guerraoui, Herlihy and Pochon~\cite{guerraoui2005}, Sections 3--4, establish a priority-based abort/wait manager with bounded-delay progress and a makespan guarantee against an offline scheduler. Diegues and Romano~\cite{diegues2014}, Sections 4--6 and its errata, adapt attempt budgets and capacity-abort handling using per-atomic-block measurements in GCC. Thus theoretically justified contention control and workload-oblivious adaptive fallback are prior contributions. Their transaction progress/cycle objectives differ from the persistent-preparation operations and hard local/shared count contracts here; their measurements do not calibrate our call toll.

\paragraph{Synchronization synthesis and robust control.}
\v{C}ern\'y et al.~\cite{cerny} choose retries, guards and caches in partial concurrent programs with a scheduler and weighted limit-average safety objective. Their Theorem 6 uses quantitative probabilistic bisimulation; the erratum corrects complexity to PSPACE. Later work optimizes protected-statement counts and statistically profiles acquisition/contention~\cite{cernyquality}; preemption-safety synthesis also addresses deadlock-free lock placement~\cite{cernysafe}. Our archived scalar potential concerns initial worst total cost, not source equivalence or intermediate-state quotients. Finite $n,B,r$ instances can be encoded as general games; we do not claim such tools cannot express them.

Mannor et al.~\cite{mannor} supply global-deviation-budget DP; Postek et al.~\cite{postek} provide finite-candidate DP and causal budgeted routing. Information-state minimax control~\cite{dave} is a general framework for the repair stopping problem after proving its least-consistent-budget reduction. Our extra repair argument uses independently realizable intervals and monotonicity to reduce a feasibility tree to one minimum-write bad path. Generic backward induction and sufficient information are not new claims.

\paragraph{Incremental computation and repair costs.}
Mokhov, Mitchell and Peyton Jones~\cite{mokhov2018}, Definitions 2.1/3.1 and Sections 4--6, classify build schedulers and rebuilders using persistent dependency/value information; Section 6.6 explicitly bounds iterative execution. Reuse, restarting and finite iteration are inherited concepts. Ley-Wild, Acar and Fluet~\cite{leywild2009}, Theorems 4--5, relate consistent change propagation to trace distance and its cost. Repair-cost semantics is therefore also prior work. Our additional question is when to accept an observed dirty set or spend a limited call on fresh preparation, against an unknown external-write budget. Their stated store-consistency/minimality and trace-distance guarantees do not give this acceptance value or the common-policy existence boundary.

\paragraph{Inherited auxiliary results.}
Top-weight sums and allocation use familiar budgeted-uncertainty and resource-allocation primitives~\cite{bertsimas,federgruen}. Our bounds identify the operation-dependent write costs that activate protected work; these subproblems are not themselves new.

The zero-fee snapshot~\cite{artifact} is an \emph{unpublished, unreviewed author artifact}. Its floor/cap lemma, two-fee ordering and restricted hardness concern supplied-price compilation. These auxiliary results and all adverse outcomes remain archived; the present theorems concern resource frontiers and partial-repair information boundaries.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limits}

The exact resource frontier concerns mandatory whole kernels, persistent outputs and the listed completing calls. It does not include replenished write budgets, structural change, exceptions, external completion or retained guards. In retained charged-guard studies, five of 384 inputs improve over serial calls (including 242 versus 243); an intermediate state separates immediate completion/release from retaining the guard (43 versus 45). General retained-guard equality remains unproved. The counterexamples and repair theorems delimit this interface.

Roslyn experiments cover selected source paths and authored changes. Same-text deadlocks and missing-document timeouts show why replayability does not imply safe protected execution. Event, snapshot and compiler projections do not establish arbitrary concurrent equivalence; direct/workspace compilations share the compiler engine. Rebase retains merge cost and is outside whole-kernel optimality. Our component profiles and call tolls have not been calibrated to that merge implementation. Footprint profiling may be exponential, and native validation can reveal different information.

Measurements use one author workstation and authored arrivals. Instrumentation and cache/JIT effects prevent inference of production timing; human benefit is unmeasured. Sparse scalar gains, failed timing calibration and expensive compilation remain adverse results. Finite tests and AI-assisted proof inspection are not mechanical proofs or independent reproductions.

\section{Conclusion}

Retry allowances determine protected-work guarantees only together with completing operations. Cached completion permits one policy to meet a universal call cap and the exact whole-kernel protection frontier for every hidden write bound. Partial repair and call costs yield distinct information boundaries. Source checks and arrival experiments expose realizable count tradeoffs and limits on their timing interpretation.
