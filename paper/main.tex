\title{What Bounded Retries Can Guarantee: Calls, Protected Work, and Partial Repair}
\author{Anonymous Author(s)}
\renewcommand{\shortauthors}{Anonymous Author(s)}
\begin{document}
\begin{abstract}
A retry limit bounds failed calls, but fallback may move all remaining computation under protection. For specified completion operations on $n$ persistent whole-kernel jobs, we derive exact frontiers under $n+r$ calls. Cached completion attains the best protected-work curve for every hidden external-write bound $B$; without it, a universally capped policy can be forced to protect all work at $B=r$. For partial repair with no call charge and positive retry allowance, one policy is optimal for all budgets exactly when each job can suffer maximum damage from one write. Call charges and precedence change this boundary. A finite construction finds a policy with the least worst-case cost excess over all informed budget-specific optima, including when exact common optimality fails. Java realizes the whole-kernel interface under stated assumptions. Roslyn modifications preserve checked events, snapshots and compiler projections, while stronger rebase operations retain unmodeled merge costs. Authored arrivals show mixed timing effects. These results identify operation-specific resource guarantees and information requirements.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

In optimistic update loops, a caller prepares outside protection and validates before publication. Switching to protected execution after finitely many failures bounds failed calls, but can protect all remaining computation. Which bounds on calls and protected work can be met together, and which completion operations are necessary?

Bounded speculation followed by locking is established practice. glibc's enabled x86 elision path permits at most three transaction starts before falling back to normal locking; HotSpot exposes \texttt{RTMRetryCount}~\cite{glibcElision,hotspotRTM}. HTM can abort without a write and discards speculative work, so it motivates finite attempt budgets but does not instantiate our retained-preparation model. We study validate-and-complete interfaces with per-update, shared-batch or combined retry allowances. Local allowances sum to a batch cap; a shared allowance permits redistribution (Proposition~\ref{prop:localcaps}). These are authored design requirements, with no assumed latency SLA.

Consider dependent transformations with works 8 and 16 and a two-call cap. Each call must complete a job. Fresh completion protects all 24 units. Cached completion prepares outside, then reuses or recomputes within one completing callback. Its worst protected work is 0, 16 or 24 at write bounds 0, 1 or 2, without receiving that bound (Figure~\ref{fig:modes}). At one write, however, worst total work rises to 40 from fresh completion's 24.

For works $(1,2,3,5)$, a six-call cap allows $r=2$ failures. Cached completion protects at most eight units at four writes; without it, a universally capped policy may protect all eleven at two writes. Necessity is relative to the specified operations. Protected work contributes to shared-exclusion blocking, but excludes waiting and predicts no latency. Section~\ref{sec:eval} isolates this contribution with a shared-bin probe and reports mixed arrival timings.

Our results concern call/protection frontiers, repair information boundaries and minimum uniform loss, assessed in Java/Roslyn. Fallback, contention control, synchronization choices and robust DP predate them~\cite{kung,attiya2006,alistarh2018,cerny,cernyquality,mannor}.

The curve needs no estimated write bound; a fixed protection ceiling does. Source checks do not establish adoption.

\section{Completion Interfaces and Resource Contracts}
\label{sec:interface}

Write $\N=\{0,1,\ldots\}$. Jobs $J=\{1,\ldots,n\}$, $n\ge1$, form a finite DAG with predecessor set $\operatorname{Pred}(i)$. Readiness requires all predecessors completed. Completion records an exact immutable output and persists despite later external writes. The objective is one successful foreground transformation per job, not a simultaneous snapshot or transaction. Policies are deterministic and causal; no randomized expected-cost guarantee is claimed.

Each ready job has a pure, opaque, whole kernel receiving its own-input identity and exact completed predecessor outputs. Every execution performs its fixed work $w_i>0$, whether inside or outside protection. Partial/incremental evaluation, preparation before readiness and discharge by external work are excluded in this interface. A preparation is tied to its own-input identity and predecessor tuple. Captured outputs cannot be reconstructed by later reads of the mutable map.

\paragraph{States, histories and turns.}
Write a state as $(x,\mathcal M,\mathcal R,\ell)$: live own-input identities $x$, the partial map $\mathcal M$ of completed jobs to immutable outputs, a finite snapshot/record store $\mathcal R$, and program-local state $\ell$. A record contains its job, captured identity, predecessor tuple and computed result. Records may be retained, copied and selected, but not fabricated or retagged without the corresponding computation. In Table~\ref{tab:calls}, $\U$ means no selected record, $\C$ a selected record, possibly stale, and $\D$ permanent completion. Failed cheap calls clear the selection, leaving retained copies available. After every finite history, each unfinished job admits a legal own-key write with an identity never used for that job. Writes preserve completed outputs.

A finite visible history $h$ lists program actions, returned values and completion events. The program chooses a ready job and operation, or an unprotected inspection, preparation or local record operation. After that choice, the environment chooses a finite word of legal writes, then the operation executes. This environment move is the \emph{gate}; it precedes comparison even within a callback. The mode/job is fixed before the gate; any later record selection uses only the prior store. Comparison and completing computation/publication are indivisible to writes; protection ends on return. Inspection honestly returns the live entry. Preparation computes a whole kernel from a captured immutable snapshot and completed parents; old captured snapshots are also allowed. Fixing the capture point, its pure body has no live read or visible interaction. Writes after capture can be replayed in order at the next gate, preserving the returned record and every subsequent observation. Only that pure body is contracted; capture is not moved.

Let $W$ count all foreground kernel work, $L$ its protected part, and $Q$ validation, publication and completion calls, including failures. Preparations, initialization and external-writer calls are excluded from $Q$. These are separate resources. Common call overhead is excluded from $L$. Proposition~\ref{prop:fulltoll} charges it explicitly in this interface; Section~\ref{sec:toll} treats the distinct repair interface. Let $\Omega_k(T)$ sum the largest $\min(k,|T|)$ works in $T$. Write $\Omega(T)$ for the total; omitted sets mean $J$. Set $\Omega_0=0$. These aggregate/allocation objects are inherited~\cite{bertsimas,federgruen}.

A \emph{cheap} call publishes on a match and returns without completion on a mismatch. Conditional publication and validation-only callbacks have this same resource transition; their Java implementations appear in Section~\ref{sec:java}. The label concerns kernel work, not elapsed time. Two modes provide cheap publication and fresh completion; three also provide cached completion. Failed validation leaves the live entry unchanged. Inspection and local record operations cost no $Q,W,L$ and never publish or retain a guard. Direct acquire/release, protection surviving return, cross-job callbacks and outside completion are absent from this entrypoint contract. These restrictions define the comparison; they do not assert completeness for arbitrary Java code or lock APIs. Each selected operation terminates. A play that never completes all jobs, including infinite free actions, has infinite objective; fairness of selected operations is an explicit assumption.

\paragraph{Strategies and admissibility.}
An informed strategy is a deterministic function $P(B,h)$; an unaware strategy is $P(h)$. Let $\mathcal E_b$ contain gate strategies using at most $b$ writes along every play. For mode family $j$, $\mathcal P_j(B,r)$ consists of strategies completing with $Q\le n+r$ against every $E\in\mathcal E_B$. By contrast, $\mathcal U_j(r)$ consists of one $B$-independent strategy satisfying that cap for every $b\ge0$ and $E\in\mathcal E_b$. Every finite-write play against a fixed program is preserved by capping its environment at that play's write count. Worst cost at budget $B$ is $\sup_{E\in\mathcal E_B}L(P,E)$; the two minima use different admissible classes.

\begin{lemma}[Fresh-write and finite-adversary facts]
\label{lem:inspection}
In Section~\ref{sec:interface}'s game, a fresh own-key write at a selected $i$-operation's gate invalidates every retained $i$-record. Any concrete prefix has both a no-write and a fresh-write continuation whenever that extra write respects the environment's budget. An adversary bounded by $b$ writes on every play forces any $P\in\mathcal P_j(b,r)$ to finish every job, regardless of free inspections or record retention.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Every retained $i$-record carries an identity previously captured from $i$; the fresh write uses an identity outside that set. Copying or selecting records cannot change this. All choices before that gate, including inspection results and the selected job/mode, coincide in the two continuations. Only the next environment move differs, so no prior observation removes the fresh-write continuation. This does not erase the observation history or assert an observation-free normal form.

A globally $b$-bounded adversary belongs to $\mathcal E_b$. A play on which the program indefinitely inspects, prepares, or works on other jobs while leaving $i$ unfinished violates admissibility on that very finite-write play. Consequently each job eventually completes. Its completing operation is either a fresh callback or a comparison using a prior record, exhaustively by Table~\ref{tab:calls}. In the first case its whole kernel is protected without a write; in the second the fresh-write argument applies. No truncation of an unbounded adversary is used.
\end{proof}

\section{Universal Call and Protected-Work Guarantees}
\label{sec:resources}

For unfinished jobs $S$, let $\mathcal A(S)=\{i\in S:\operatorname{Pred}(i)\cap S=\varnothing\}$ denote the ready jobs.

\begin{theorem}[Protected work under a call cap]
\label{thm:resources}
In Section~\ref{sec:interface}'s interface, fix integers $B,r\ge0$. Among programs supplied with $B$ that complete all jobs with $Q\le n+r$ in every environment with at most $B$ writes, the minimum worst protected work is
\[
 L_3^*(B,r)=\Omega_{\min((B-r)_+,n)},\qquad
 L_2^*(B,r)=\begin{cases}0,&B\le r,\\\Omega,&B>r,\end{cases}
\]
with and without cached completion, respectively. Any DAG is permitted.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
At most $r$ calls may fail to complete. Freshly prepared cheap attempts have disjoint read-to-comparison intervals; each failure needs a distinct write. If $B\le r$ they give $Q\le n+B$, $L=0$. Otherwise switch after $r$ failures to cached completion: at most $B-r$ writes remain, giving the stated bound. All-protected gives the two-mode upper bound.

For the two-mode lower bound when $B>r$, reject each cheap call with a fresh own-key write, stopping writes after the $(r+1)$st rejection. This uses at most $r+1\le B$ writes. An admissible program cannot incur that many noncompleting calls, so all its jobs complete under protection.

For three modes, put $k=\min(B-r,n)>0$ and fix the $k$ heaviest jobs as targets. The adversary maintains $f$, the number of noncompleting target comparisons, and $p$, the number of completed targets. Initially both are zero. It writes only at a target's comparison gate, with a fresh identity, while $f\le r$ and $p<k$. It stops all writes permanently when $f=r+1$ or $p=k$. Counters follow actual outcomes: $f$ increases on a noncompleting target comparison and $p$ on every target completion. While writing is active, cheap target calls fail and cached ones recompute. Fresh completion protects $w_i$ without a write. After stopping, calls follow their ordinary semantics.

Let $v$ be the writes so far. At each program node, after the preceding operation returns, induction gives $v\le f+p$: each write led to a failure or a cached completion; fresh completion only increases $p$. Immediately before any further write, $f\le r$ and $p\le k-1$, giving $v+1\le f+p+1\le r+k\le B$. Once stopped, no write is added. Thus this is unconditionally an $\mathcal E_B$ strategy, including plays that violate the call cap.

For an admissible program, $f=r+1$ is impossible: completion requires $n$ completing calls in addition to those failures, already exceeding $n+r$. Thus $f\le r$ throughout its play. Lemma~\ref{lem:inspection} forces every target eventually to complete; each such completion is fresh or is a cached mismatch, both protecting its whole work. Consequently $p=k$ and $L\ge\Omega_k$. Free inspections, stale selections and retained copies cannot evade either case. Predecessors still require their own completing calls, already included in the mandatory $n$. A nontarget predecessor changes neither $f$ nor $p$ and receives no write from this adversary. A target predecessor is counted exactly once in $p$. Thus dependencies delay targets without changing the write or call accounting.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[One policy for every writer budget]
\label{thm:universal}
Let $\mathcal U_j(r)$ contain the $j$-mode programs that receive no $B$ and complete with $Q\le n+r$ in every finite-write environment. Set $U_j(B,r)=\inf_{P\in\mathcal U_j(r)}\sup_{\#\mathrm{writes}\le B}L(P,E)$. Then
\[
 U_3(B,r)=L_3^*(B,r),\qquad U_2(B,r)=\Omega\,\mathbf1[B\ge r].
\]
One policy per mode family attains these values simultaneously for all $B$ and positive works on any DAG, using only readiness and a global cheap-failure counter.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Switch after $r$ cheap failures to a fresh read/preparation with cached completion (three) or to fresh protected completion (two). This uses at most $r$ extra calls universally. At $B<r$ no protection is needed; at $B=r$ cached completions after switching still match. Theorem~\ref{thm:resources} supplies the remaining upper and three-mode lower bounds.

For two modes when $B\ge r$, reject every cheap call with a fresh own-key write until $r$ rejections, then stop. Only protected calls complete before this. Afterwards, another rejectable call could fail in a different finite environment, violating the call cap. Thus all jobs complete under protection with at most $r\le B$ writes. That additional hypothetical write is absent from the lower-bound execution. Retained preparations and inspections cannot prevent a fresh write before comparison.
\end{proof}

For equal works and $B>r$, the ratio is $n/\min(B-r,n)$. At $B=r$, only an informed two-mode program knows no write remains and may risk another cheap call. The unaware class must also be safe in extensions with another write, causing the boundary shift. A deployment that supplies a certified bound and requires the cap only within it instead uses $\mathcal P_j(B,r)$; that is a different information contract.

Readiness constrains when a target is attempted, but persistence requires every target eventually. Thus DAG edges do not change this frontier. They become consequential with repair operations and call costs (Section~\ref{sec:tolldag}).

\subsection{Local Caps and a Shared Cap Can Be Required Together}

Suppose job $i$ may incur at most $r_i$ noncompleting calls, and the batch at most $s\le R=\sum_i r_i$. These caps hold in every finite-write environment. Define the weighted subset value
\[
 A_{\boldsymbol c}(B)=\max_{T\subseteq J:\,\sum_{i\in T}c_i\le B}\sum_{i\in T}w_i,
\]
allowing zero-cost items. This is a knapsack value; the contribution is identifying the write cost that activates each job's protection.
\begin{proposition}[Local and shared resource contracts]
\label{prop:localcaps}
The minimum worst protected work under both caps is
\[
\begin{aligned}
 U_2(B;\boldsymbol r,s)&=\max\{A_{\boldsymbol r}(B),\Omega\,\mathbf1[B\ge s]\},\\
 U_3(B;\boldsymbol r,s)&=\max\{A_{\boldsymbol r+\boldsymbol1}(B),\Omega_{(B-s)_+}\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
One policy attains each curve simultaneously for every $B$: switch to guaranteed completion when either the current local allowance or the shared allowance is exhausted. Its worst call count is $n+\min(B,s)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For a local lower bound, target a maximizing subset. Reject each target's cheap calls up to its local cap. In two modes another rejectable call is then universally inadmissible, so the target completes protectively using at most $r_i$ actual writes. In three modes, one additional write invalidates its eventual cached comparison; an earlier fresh completion already pays $w_i$. Thus each target costs at most $r_i+1$ writes. The shared lower bounds follow from Theorem~\ref{thm:universal}, since adding local constraints cannot enlarge its policy class.

For the switching policy, if fewer than $s$ failures occur, a protected job has exhausted its own cap: it requires $r_i$ writes in two modes, and $r_i+1$ in three. If $s$ failures occur, two modes pay at most $\Omega$; in three, every protected completion still needs its own additional mismatch write, so at most $(B-s)_+$ jobs are protected. These two cases give the maximum above. A fresh preparation after every rejection makes the intervals disjoint. Distributing $\min(B,s)$ cheap failures among the local allowances attains the call bound; its maximum need not coincide with the protection maximum.
\end{proof}

With only local caps, $s=R$ gives $U_2=A_{\boldsymbol r}(B)$ and $U_3=A_{\boldsymbol r+\boldsymbol1}(B)$. Uniform $r_i=r\ge1$ yields $\Omega_{\lfloor B/r\rfloor}$ and $\Omega_{\lfloor B/(r+1)\rfloor}$, with $Q\le n+nr$; at $r=0$, the values are $\Omega$ and $\Omega_B$, with $Q=n$. Reusing a rejected preparation loses the extra-write requirement. Sharing allowance $R$ permits weakly less worst protection than reserving it locally.

\subsection{Choosing Speculation When Every Call Has a Cost}

Let each counted call cost $\kappa\ge0$ and minimize $C=L+\kappa Q$. Let $C^*(B,r)$ be the minimum worst cost over the full $B$-informed program class of Theorem~\ref{thm:resources}, with only the shared cap. Mode choice remains \emph{before} comparison; the partial-repair interface below allows a different observation/action order. Write $C(P,B)$ for policy $P$'s worst cost at budget $B$.

\begin{proposition}[Loss of the global-threshold policy]
\label{prop:fulltoll}
The three-mode policy $P_r$ that freshly prepares before each attempt and switches after $r$ cheap failures to cached completion has
\[
\begin{aligned}
 C(P_r,B)&=n\kappa+\kappa\min(B,r)+\Omega_{(B-r)_+},\\
 0\le C(P_r,B)-C^*(B,r)&\le\kappa\min(B,r)\le r\kappa.
\end{aligned}
\]
The $r\kappa$ bound is sharp for this policy.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The call and protection upper bounds follow from Theorem~\ref{thm:universal}. They are attained together: reject the first $\min(B,r)$ cheap calls, then use remaining writes to invalidate cached completions of the heaviest jobs. Every competitor has a resource lower-bound execution with both $L\ge\Omega_{(B-r)_+}$ and $Q\ge n$. Thus $C^*\ge n\kappa+\Omega_{(B-r)_+}$ on that execution. For one job and $B=r+1$, invalidating every comparison forces protected completion. Immediate cached completion is optimal at $\kappa+w$, while $P_r$ pays $(r+1)\kappa+w$.
\end{proof}

The bound compares a specified policy with an informed comparator. A caller may instead want the best possible loss without ever learning $B$. For one job this question has an exact answer.
\begin{proposition}[Exact one-job minimax additive loss]
\label{prop:regret}
For one job with work $w>0$, let $P$ range over all deterministic programs that receive no $B$ and complete with $Q\le r+1$ in every finite-write environment. Then
\[
 \inf_P\sup_{B\ge0}\bigl(C(P,B)-C^*(B,r)\bigr)
 =\min\{(w-\kappa)_+,r\kappa\}.
\]
Always-cached completion attains the first term for $r\ge1$; $P_r$ attains the second. Choose the smaller, without estimating $B$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The informed one-job value is $\kappa+\min(B\kappa,w)$ for $B\le r$, and $\kappa+w$ for $B>r$. For the first case, cheap attempts and immediate cached completion give the two upper bounds. For a lower bound, count all noncompleting calls in $a$. While $a<B$, invalidate each selected usable comparison with at most one fresh write. Actual writes are at most $a$; a cached completion can therefore be invalidated within $a+1\le B$ writes. Fresh completion already pays $w$. If $a=B$ is reached, stop writing: any subsequent completion has at least $B+1$ calls, regardless of its mode. For $B>r$, invalidating through the cap uses at most $r+1\le B$ writes and forces protected completion.

Now consider an unaware program with $r\ge1$ and $0<\kappa<w$. Again invalidate each chosen usable comparison, and let $a\le r$ count all noncompleting calls before its first fresh/cached completion. If fresh, witness budget $B=a$ gives regret at least $a\kappa+w-\min(a\kappa,w)\ge w$. If cached and $a<r$, one more write and $B=a+1$ give regret at least $a\kappa+w-\min((a+1)\kappa,w)\ge w-\kappa$. If cached and $a=r$, use $B=r+1$ to get $r\kappa$. These are upper bounds on actual writes, not assumed equalities. Stop the adversary after at most $r+1$ noncompleting calls; the cap and finite-write completion exclude an infinite avoidance run. Fresh identities invalidate retained preparations after mode selection, so free inspections do not change the argument.

Always-cached has worst regret $(w-\kappa)_+$ for $r\ge1$. Substituting the informed curve above into Proposition~\ref{prop:fulltoll} gives worst regret $r\kappa$ for $P_r$. Nonnegativity covers $r=0$, $\kappa=0$ and $\kappa\ge w$.
\end{proof}

This specializes classical cost balancing~\cite{karlin1988} and capped rent-or-buy~\cite{lotker2012}. For a serial threshold $t\le r$, subtracting the inevitable completion toll gives cost $B\kappa$ for $B\le t$ and $t\kappa+w$ otherwise. Beyond $B=r$, the call cap also forces the informed comparator to complete protectively. Threshold selection and regret are inherited ideas; the proof additionally covers inspections, retained records and fresh completion. When $0<\kappa<w$, immediate cached completion minimizes loss iff $w\le(r+1)\kappa$; otherwise use all retries. With $w=8,r=2$, tolls 1 and 3 select retries and immediate completion, respectively. The units are supplied costs; general whole-kernel workflow minimax loss remains open. The repair interface below has a separate finite solution.

\section{When Partial Repair Makes the Budget Valuable}
\label{sec:partial}

Whole-kernel recomputation makes one invalidation as damaging as many. We next study an interface that reuses unaffected components, and characterize when partial damage destroys a common optimum. Roslyn merges are not assumed to satisfy this cost law.

\paragraph{Repair interface and observations.}
One job consists of $m$ components with positive weights $w_c$. A write changes fresh versions on one nonempty footprint from a fixed finite family $\Gamma$. Footprints may overlap and repeat; each occurrence costs one write. Outside protection, preparation reads and computes every component anew. A protected call observes the dirty set $D$, the union of footprints since that preparation. It either accepts, repairs exactly $D$ and publishes the job, or rejects and prepares again outside. An empty observation can complete with no repair. No partial publication, free dirty-set query, retained protection or cheaper completion operation is available. A fresh whole-job completion costs at least repairing any dirty set. Calls and preparations terminate.

The turn order is preparation, an environment word of footprints, observation $D$, then the accept/reject choice within that same counted call. No write intervenes between observation and decision. A visible history lists job choices, observations and decisions, including current $D$; repeated writes and word lengths are hidden. Informed strategies additionally receive $B$. Completion is required within $q\ge1$ calls in every finite-write execution. One preparation per call permits at most $q$ preparations. The objective is protected repair work, excluding outside preparation and common call overhead. Let
\[
G(b)=\max_{0\le t\le b,\ \gamma_j\in\Gamma}
       \sum_{c\in\gamma_1\cup\cdots\cup\gamma_t}w_c,
\qquad W^*=w\!\left(\bigcup\Gamma\right).
\]
Then $G(0)=0$, $G$ is nondecreasing and subadditive, and $G(m)=W^*>0$. Unreachable components do not contribute to $W^*$. Singleton footprints give the prefix sums of weights sorted in decreasing order. A general footprint family need not have a cheap profile: computing these unions is part of the input-processing cost.

\begin{theorem}[Exact value and information boundary for repair]
\label{thm:repair}
For a controller supplied with $B$, minimum worst repair work is
\[
K_q(B)=G(\lfloor B/q\rfloor).
\]
Among controllers receiving no $B$ that attain $K_q(B)=0$ for every $B<q$, the least possible worst-work envelope is
\[
T_q(B)=G((B-q+1)_+).
\]
One policy attains this envelope for all $B$. For $q\ge2$, a single controller is optimal for every $B$ if and only if $G(1)=W^*$. For $q=1$, such a controller always exists.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put $k=\lfloor B/q\rfloor$. The aware policy accepts work at most $G(k)$ and otherwise rejects while a call remains. An observation with greater work requires at least $k+1$ writes. Rejecting $q-1$ such observations exhausts all opportunities for a $q$th: $q(k+1)>B$. Thus forced acceptance on the last call still meets $G(k)$. This policy also satisfies the call cap outside its supplied budget, where the work guarantee is not asserted. Conversely, a word of at most $k$ footprints realizes $G(k)$. Repeat it after every preparation. Any accepting call pays $G(k)$, and $q$ repetitions use at most $qk\le B$ writes. Fresh whole-job completion is no cheaper.

For the unaware lower bound, write once in each of the first $q-1$ preparation intervals. After observation $j<q$, the history is compatible with only $j$ writes in total. Accepting positive damage, or fresh completion, would violate that budget's zero optimum. Hence the policy must reject all $q-1$ times. In its final interval spend up to $B-q+1$ writes realizing $G(B-q+1)$. No retry remains. The matching policy rejects its first $q-1$ nonempty observations, then accepts; earlier empty observations complete for zero work.

Every simultaneous optimum meets the preceding zero-work requirements. If $G(1)<W^*$, let $k\ge2$ be the first index with $G(k)>G(1)$, and set $B=q-1+k$. Then $1\le\lfloor B/q\rfloor<k$, so $K_q(B)=G(1)<G(k)=T_q(B)$. Conversely, if $G(1)=W^*$, both expressions are zero for $B<q$ and $W^*$ otherwise. For $q=1$ their arguments coincide.
\end{proof}

The boundary concerns write damage: a footprint covering all reachable work preserves simultaneous optimality. With separate-component writes, further damage can make budget knowledge change the optimal acceptance threshold.

For two unit components, singleton writes and $q=2$, $K_2(B)$ at $B=0,1,2,3$ is $0,0,1,1$. Optimality at $B=1$ forces an unaware policy to reject its first dirty component. Two subsequent writes make both components dirty; its last call costs 2 at $B=3$. The aware policy at that budget accepts one dirty component immediately and retries only two-component damage. \paragraph{Quantifying the loss without a budget.}
For repair work alone, a finite competitive ratio requires zero work when $K_q(B)=0$. Theorem~\ref{thm:repair} therefore identifies the exact optimal ratio as $\sup_{B\ge q}T_q(B)/K_q(B)$. Subadditivity gives an upper bound $q$: with $k=\lfloor B/q\rfloor$, $B-q+1\le qk$, so $G(B-q+1)\le qG(k)$. It is sharp for sufficiently many unit singleton components at $B=2q-1$, where the ratio is $q$. Thus the loss can grow with the call allowance; it is not a constant-price perturbation of whole-kernel optimality.

\subsection{A Shared Retry Allowance Across a Workflow}
\label{sec:partialdag}

The same boundary extends beyond a single job. Consider $n$ persistent jobs with a DAG, each with its own component/footprint family. A ready job is freshly prepared before each attempted observation; no other preparation is retained. A write affects the currently attempted job, with no cross-job footprint. After a rejection the next ready job may be selected anew. The total call cap is $n+r$, with a shared $r$; acceptance pays that job's dirty-component work and permanently completes it. Write $W_i=G_i(\infty)$, $\sigma_i=\min\{b:G_i(b)=W_i\}$, $\Sigma=\sum_i\sigma_i$, and $W^*=\sum_iW_i$. Let $K(B)$ be the known-budget minimum worst repair work for this interface.

\begin{theorem}[Workflow information boundary]
\label{thm:repairdag}
For $r\ge1$, one policy simultaneously attains $K(B)$ for every $B$ iff $G_i(1)=W_i$ for every job. This holds both for a fixed order and for choices among DAG-ready jobs. Under the condition,
\[
K(B)=\text{sum of the largest }\min((B-r)_+,n)\text{ values }W_i.
\]
For $r=0$, accepting every observation is simultaneously optimal for arbitrary footprints. More generally, the first budget at which $K(B)=W^*$ is
\[
B_{\rm sat}=\Sigma+r\max_i\sigma_i.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If every job has a one-write maximum, reject the first $r$ nonempty observations globally and otherwise accept. Early acceptances are clean; after $r$ rejections, at most $(B-r)_+$ jobs can accept positive damage. To force the matching lower bound, target that many heaviest jobs, writing once before each target observation to cause maximum damage. A target completion costs $W_i$, and at most $r$ rejections add writes, so $r+k\le B$ writes suffice for $k$ targets. An attempted $(r+1)$st rejection would itself violate the call cap within budget; truncate the adversary there. Selection and precedence do not avoid completing the targets.

For necessity, all $K(B)$ with $B\le r$ are zero. A simultaneous optimum must therefore reject when one write is issued before each of its first $r$ observations: after observation $t$, the history is compatible with only $t\le r$ writes in total. No job has yet completed. Now spend $\sigma_i$ writes to saturate each job on its next attempt. No rejection remains, so the policy pays $W^*$ with $B_0=\Sigma+r$ writes. If some $\sigma_j\ge2$, a competing policy reserves all $r$ rejections for fully dirty observations of job $j$ and accepts everything else. Full total work would require $\Sigma+r\sigma_j>B_0$ writes, as every invocation uses a fresh preparation. Its worst cost is therefore strictly below $W^*$; the attainable cost set is finite. This contradiction proves necessity even in a prescribed order.

At $r=0$, every call must complete and acceptance dominates fresh whole-job completion. The value is $\max_{\sum b_i\le B}\sum_iG_i(b_i)$ independently of order. For the final statement, saturating every attempted observation uses at most $\Sigma+r\max_i\sigma_i$ writes on an admissible run and forces full work. Below that budget, reserve all $r$ rejections for full observations of a maximum-$\sigma_i$ job. Full work would require exactly that many writes, so the worst cost is strictly smaller.
\end{proof}

For two and one unit singleton components, $r=1$ gives $K(4)=2$, yet an unaware policy optimal at $B=1$ can be forced to pay 3 with four writes. Intermediate curves generally require the finite game.

\subsection{Charging Every Call Changes the Boundary Again}
\label{sec:toll}

Repair work omits the protected cost of validation itself. To expose this assumption, charge $\kappa\ge0$ on \emph{each} call and a common $\mu\ge0$ once upon completion. Acceptance on call $t$ costs $\mu+t\kappa+w(D)$. Preparation remains outside this metric. Let $M$ be the sorted multiset of $qm$ numbers
\[
\{\,t\kappa+G(k):1\le t\le q,\ 0\le k<m\,\},
\]
with one-based entries $M_j$ and repeated entries retained.

\begin{theorem}[Toll curve and simultaneous-optimality boundary]
\label{thm:toll}
The known-budget optimum is $\mu+C_q(B)$, where
\[
C_q(B)=\begin{cases}
\min\{\kappa+W^*,M_{B+1}\},& B<qm,\\
\kappa+W^*,&B\ge qm.
\end{cases}
\]
For $q\ge2$, simultaneous optimality holds exactly in the following cases: $\kappa=0$ and $G(1)=W^*$, or $\kappa\ge W^*$. It fails for every $0<\kappa<W^*$. The common cost $\mu$ does not change these conditions. At $q=1$, first acceptance is always simultaneously optimal.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Every completed play pays $\mu$ exactly once and rejects pay none, so remove that common constant. Fix $T<\kappa+W^*$. On call $t$, an unacceptable dirty set has $t\kappa+w(D)>T$. Its least write cost is
$a_t(T)=\min\{k:t\kappa+G(k)>T\}$. Such an index exists since $G(m)=W^*$. A sequence of $q$ unacceptable observations is possible exactly when $\sum_ta_t(T)\le B$: each interval can independently realize a minimum-cost witness. Otherwise accepting the first acceptable observation finishes within $q$ calls and cost $T$. Because $G$ is nondecreasing, $a_t(T)$ counts indices $0\le k<m$ with $t\kappa+G(k)\le T$. The omitted $k=m$ term exceeds $T$ for every $t$. Thus $\sum_ta_t(T)$ counts all entries of $M$ at most $T$, including ties. Feasibility requires this count to exceed $B$, first occurring at $M_{B+1}$ if below the first-accept cap. For $B\ge qm$, the count cannot exceed $B$, leaving that cap. This proves both the index and endpoint.

If $\kappa>0$ and a policy ever rejects a first observation, follow it by maximum damage until it accepts. At a sufficiently large finite budget it pays at least $2\kappa+W^*$, exceeding the aware first-accept cap $\kappa+W^*$. Thus a simultaneous optimum must accept every first observation. Let $k^*$ be the first index with $G(k^*)=W^*$. With known budget $k^*$, accept all first observations except maximum damage; reject that one and the next observation is necessarily clean. Its worst cost is at most $\max\{\kappa+W_{<},2\kappa\}$, where $W_{<}<W^*$ is the largest attainable nonmaximum damage. For $0<\kappa<W^*$ this is strictly smaller than the first-accept value $\kappa+W^*$. Conversely, when $\kappa\ge W^*$, presenting a first observation with damage $G(B)$ forces either $\kappa+G(B)$ on acceptance or at least $2\kappa$ on rejection. First acceptance attains the former, which is no larger. The zero-toll case is Theorem~\ref{thm:repair}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{With Call Charges, Precedence and History Matter}
\label{sec:tolldag}

Return to the workflow interface of Section~\ref{sec:partialdag}, charging $\kappa>0$ per call. Write $K^{\kappa}(B)$ for its known-budget value, $H(B)=\max_{\sum b_i\le B}\sum_iG_i(b_i)$ and $C_{\max}=n\kappa+\sum_iW_i$.

\begin{theorem}[Call charges in workflows]
\label{thm:tolldag}
For independent jobs and $r\ge1$, a simultaneous optimum exists iff $\kappa\ge\max_iW_i$. This condition is sufficient on every DAG: first acceptance has exact value $n\kappa+H(B)$. On a general DAG, a sink with $W_i>\kappa$ prevents simultaneous optimality. The condition on all jobs is no longer necessary.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For sufficiency, fix an allocation attaining $H(B)$. Present its dirty set on each job's first attempt and leave that job clean after a rejection. Accepting pays the allocated damage; rejecting incurs an extra toll at least as large. This matches the first-accept upper bound, regardless of order.

For necessity, a simultaneous optimum cannot reject after a prefix whose completed jobs were all fully dirty: saturating every remaining attempt would then force cost above $C_{\max}$ at some finite budget. Hence fully dirty first observations force it to pay $C_{\max}$ with $\Sigma$ writes. If a job $j$ with $W_j>\kappa$ can be placed last, an aware policy at budget $\Sigma$ accepts preceding jobs. If any observation is less than full it accepts throughout, costing strictly below $C_{\max}$. Otherwise all $\Sigma$ necessary writes are spent; rejecting $j$ once then completing cleanly costs $C_{\max}-W_j+\kappa<C_{\max}$. The finite outcome set makes the worst cost strictly smaller. Every independent job, and every DAG sink, can be last.

For the final claim, let $A$ have singleton components of weights 1 and 2, let $C$ have one component of weight 1, require $A$ before $C$, and take $r=1,\kappa=2$. First acceptance has curve $4,6,7,8$ at $B=0,1,2,\ge3$. Matching lower bounds subtract the mandatory toll 4. At $B=1$, dirty $A$'s weight-2 component: acceptance or rejection costs at least 2. At $B=2$, do the same, then dirty $C$ after acceptance (cost 3), or the weight-2 component again after rejection (cost 4). At $B=3$, first saturate $A$ with two writes; then dirty $C$ after acceptance (cost 4), or the weight-2 component after rejection (cost 4). Thus first acceptance is a common optimum although $\kappa<W_A$. Reversing the order makes $A$ a sink and removes every common optimum.
\end{proof}

History-dependent rejection can be essential even with one-component jobs. Consider chain $(a,h,a)$, $h>a>0$, with $r=1,\kappa=a$. Accept throughout except that a dirty middle job is rejected once if the first job completed cleanly. Its exact curve is $3a,4a,4a+h,5a+h$ at $B=0,1,2,\ge3$. First acceptance instead costs $3a+h>4a$ at one write. For upper bounds, a retry leaves at most $B-1$ writes; whenever its extra toll $a$ is paid, the clean first job has saved $a$. For matching lower bounds, one dirty observation forces at least $a$ beyond baseline. With two writes, leave the first job clean and dirty the middle, then dirty the last after acceptance or the middle again after rejection, forcing $a+h$. With three writes, dirty the first job: after acceptance apply that two-write strategy to the last two jobs; after rejection dirty the middle and last instead. This forces $h+2a$. A preceding clean rejection already spends the toll and leaves no retry. No writer budget is supplied; $(a,h)=(1,3)$ gives curve $3,4,7,8$.

\paragraph{Constructing Policies and Minimizing Uniform Loss.}
A finite recurrence decides whether a common optimum exists beyond these cases. Let $c(D)$ be the minimum footprint-word length realizing observation $D$ on the attempted job. Set $E=\Sigma+r\max_i\sigma_i$; saturating every attempt proves $K^{\kappa}(b)=C_{\max}$ for $b\ge E$. Let $e$ be the least-consistent write count clipped to $E$, $t$ the remaining rejections, and $a$ the cost already paid. Define $\Theta(\varnothing,t,e)=K^{\kappa}(e)$ and, with $e_D=\min(e+c(D),E)$,
\begin{equation}
\Theta(S,t,e)=\max_{i\in\mathcal A(S)}\min_D
\max\left\{\Theta(S-i,t,e_D)-\kappa-w_i(D),\
\Theta(S,t-1,e_D)-\kappa\right\},
\label{eq:viability}
\end{equation}
omitting the rejection term at $t=0$. A continuation exists exactly when $a\le\Theta(S,t,e)$; in particular, $\Theta(J,r,0)\ge0$ decides common optimality.

To prove this, consider a completed history and sum the minimum footprint-word lengths of its observations. Fresh preparations and independent own-job writes realize those words in separate intervals, so the sum is the least budget consistent with that history. Every larger budget permits the same history by leaving writes unused. Since $K^\kappa$ is nondecreasing, meeting its value at the least budget is necessary and sufficient for that history to meet every consistent target. Clipping preserves every extension because $\min(\min(e,E)+c(D),E)=\min(e+c(D),E)$. Below $E$ it retains the exact least budget; at or above $E$ every consistent comparator target is $C_{\max}$. Future observation sets depend on the job, not the hidden remaining budget, since one policy must cover all finite budgets. Thus clipping changes neither targets nor choices, even if $c(D)>\sigma_i$. Accumulated cost $a$ is never clipped.

Induct on $|S|+t$. With no jobs left, feasibility is the interval $a\le K^\kappa(e)$. For a fixed ready job and observation, accepting adds $\kappa+w_i(D)$ and rejecting adds $\kappa$; either strictly decreases the induction measure. Each child's feasible costs therefore give a shifted downward interval. The choice after observing $D$ takes the larger endpoint. Requiring success for every observation takes the minimum; choosing a job before that observation takes the maximum. This proves Equation~\eqref{eq:viability} with its required $\exists i\,\forall D\,\exists\mathrm{action}$ order. A maximizing job and any satisfied child inequality supply an executable policy using history alone. If the initial interval excludes zero, the failed alternatives identify an adversarial finite observation path for each attempted controller choice.


For this repair interface, also at $\kappa=0$, let $C(P,B)$ denote a universally capped unaware policy's worst cost. The minimum uniform additive loss is
\[
 \delta^*=\min_P\sup_{B\ge0}\{C(P,B)-K^\kappa(B)\}
 =-\Theta(J,r,0)\ge0.
\]
For a fixed $P$, each reachable terminal history is realizable at its least consistent budget; monotonicity of $K^\kappa$ makes that budget its worst regret witness. Replacing every terminal target by $K^\kappa(e)+\delta$ shifts all thresholds by $\delta$, since minimum, maximum and constant subtraction commute with the shift. Hence a loss allowance $\delta$ is feasible iff $0\le\Theta(J,r,0)+\delta$. Shifted child inequalities extract an attaining policy. Finite job/observation alphabets and at most $n+r$ rounds make the policy minimum attained, before clipping. Fixed per-completion costs cancel from both policy and comparator.

For the preceding $A\to C$ example with $\kappa=1$, the informed curve is $2,3,5,5,6$ at $B=0,1,2,3,\ge4$. An emitted policy has curve $2,4,5,6,6$ and minimum uniform loss $\delta^*=1$. At $\kappa=2$ the loss is zero. 


For each minimum footprint count, only its maximum attainable damage is needed in the adversarial alternatives. With $I$ unfinished sets and $h=\max_i(m_i+1)$, the table has $O(I(r+1)(E+1))$ entries and uses $O(In(r+1)(E+1)h)$ arithmetic operations, given profiles and the comparator. For a fixed order, $I=n+1$ and there is one job choice. This removes accumulated-cost enumeration, not potentially exponential DAG states or footprint profiling. It is a specialization of finite minimax and information-state reasoning, not a new general dynamic-programming principle.

\section{Actual External Writes in Java}
\label{sec:java}

Use Java 17's \texttt{ConcurrentHashMap} with one foreground thread, distinct nonremoved keys, non-null immutable identity-equal values and never-reused write identities. Each job updates its fixed key using own snapshots and captured parents. Kernels and key hash/equality/comparison methods are stable, side-effect-free and normally terminating. Replacement accepts identity or \texttt{equals} equality~\cite{cmap17,chmsource17}. The API specifies atomic \texttt{compute} with one callback invocation~\cite{chm17}; the inspected source executes existing-key callbacks under a bin monitor, including tree bins~\cite{chmsource17}. Different keys may share bins.

Implement Table~\ref{tab:calls} using \texttt{replace(key,expected,result)} and \texttt{compute}. Validation-only callbacks return the prepared result on match, otherwise the unchanged entry with a failure flag; cached callbacks recompute on mismatch, and fresh callbacks always compute under protection. Capture permanent milestones locally rather than rereading the map.

Callbacks never directly or recursively modify the map, as required by the API. Our model additionally excludes other-key reads, other-job kernels and guard work; multiple preparations remain allowed. $Q$ counts API invocations, not monitor acquisitions; $L$ counts only kernel work under protection. Internal transfer/treeification preserves stored value references and adds no modeled external write or kernel work. The monitor may be released before API return: abstract postpublication internal steps away and place intervening writes at the next gate. Callbacks retain no guard across calls. Scheduling and normal call completion are assumptions, not consequences of finite writing.

\begin{theorem}[Resource bounds for actual writes]
\label{thm:writes}
Under the stated API, monitor and scheduling assumptions, Theorems~\ref{thm:resources}--\ref{thm:universal}'s exact call/protected-kernel-work bounds hold for Java programs using the listed operations, with separate or shared internal bins. This is a mathematical refinement argument for those operations, not a mechanically verified property of an entire JDK binary.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For the upper bounds, the serial policies freshly read and prepare after every cheap failure. Their read-to-comparison intervals, including cached completions, are disjoint; every mismatch contains a distinct successful write. Thus at most $B$ mismatches occur. Every fresh/cached completing call completes exactly one job, and a cached mismatch performs precisely that job's kernel inside.

For the lower bounds, use the target-job adversaries of Section~\ref{sec:resources}. Immediately before a chosen comparison acquires its monitor, a successful own-key write installs a fresh identity, invalidating every retained preparation for that job. The writer need not run inside the monitor: moving this write before acquisition preserves the selected operation and its mismatch outcome. A cheap call fails; a cached call recomputes inside; a fresh callback already incurs its whole kernel inside. Stale copies cannot complete cheaply, captured parent outputs persist, and no monitor survives the preceding call. Bin collisions therefore do not prevent the next chosen write. The prohibition on other-job work within a callback ensures it cannot complete several targets for one call. The same write counts and hypothetical extra-write argument apply.
\end{proof}

Separate counters record protected work and calls; allocation, waiting, initialization, writers and other API instructions are excluded. Scalar charges are supplied costs.

Its 1,728 arrival units give mixed batch/sequential ratios: 0.906--1.189 foreground, 0.511--1.404 background p95, 0.917--1.281 observed protected spans. A document-state guard changes four of 96 compiler-case counter rows to reuse after reference removal, preserving newer references and diagnostics. All 288 common snapshot projections agree; one fewer warm snapshot gives 2,552 project checks. Timing of this document-state-guard variant is unmeasured; protected merge work remains.

\paragraph{Comparing shared and per-update contracts.}
The local baseline resets retries before each update in unchanged patch04. Eight arms cover unmodified baseline, instrumented original, shared two/three at $r=2$ and local two/three at $r=1,2$. Eight cyclic Latin blocks balance process positions. Each process serves 32 serial updates and 64 FIFO writers with precomputed texts, no supplied budget, and intervals $d\in\{0,10,100,1000\}\,\mu$s. Two warm-up and ten measured batches per interval are shuffled before execution. All 2,560 measured and 512 warm-up units pass source checks; 12 corruptions reject. Arrivals are authored.

For an update's actual write count $B_i$, local modes satisfy $Q_i=1+f_i$, $f_i\le\min(B_i,r)$, and inside count at most $\mathbf1[B_i\ge r]$ (two) or $\mathbf1[B_i\ge r+1]$ (three). Per-update deltas sum to batch counts. All updates and batches meet Proposition~\ref{prop:localcaps}'s count bounds. \section{Related Work and Research Lineage}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Fallback and online contention management.}
Kung and Robinson~\cite{kung}, Section 3.3/p.220, already retain the critical-section semaphore during reexecution after repeated validation failure. Lock-elision retry caps likewise precede our work~\cite{glibcElision,hotspotRTM}. Cached completion here requires an unrolled-back preparation and atomic identity-check/recompute; HTM capacity or explicit aborts need not consume a write~\cite{gccRTM}. RPC retry budgets may instead cap concurrency or time-window rates and permit failure~\cite{ulrichRetries,envoyRetries}. Those controls do not impose our persistent-job completion and total-call contract.

Welc, Saha and Adl-Tabatabai~\cite{welc2008}, Sections 3.1/7.1, already preserve and protect a running transaction's prefix during irrevocable transition. Their second-abort heuristic continues revocably if token acquisition fails, giving no universal attempt cap. Prefix reuse and irrevocability are inherited; our entrypoint-specific resource curves are different guarantees.

Attiya et al.~\cite{attiya2006}, Sections 2--3/5, analyze one Greedy contention manager that does not receive the per-job non-conflict failure bound $k$. It is $O(ks)$-competitive for makespan with $s$ resources, with a corresponding deterministic lower bound under time-dependent resource requests. Unknown-disturbance control and all-policy lower bounds thus predate ours. Our bounds instead concern global external writes, universal call caps and exact protected work for persistent jobs. Alistarh et al.~\cite{alistarh2018}, Sections 4--5, optimize whether and when to abort a conflicting transaction with known abort cost and unknown remaining duration. Their throughput policy lacks progress; Section 7 adds probabilistic progress, distinct from our universal call cap.

Guerraoui, Herlihy and Pochon~\cite{guerraoui2005}, Sections 3--4, establish a priority-based abort/wait manager with bounded-delay progress and a makespan guarantee against an offline scheduler. Diegues and Romano~\cite{diegues2014}, Sections 4--6 and its errata, adapt attempt budgets and capacity-abort handling using per-atomic-block measurements in GCC. Their progress/cycle objectives differ from our persistent preparations and hard local/shared call caps; their measurements do not calibrate our toll.

\paragraph{Synchronization synthesis and robust control.}
\v{C}ern\'y et al.~\cite{cerny} choose retries, guards and caches in partial concurrent programs with a scheduler and weighted limit-average safety objective. Their Theorem 6 uses quantitative probabilistic bisimulation; the erratum corrects complexity to PSPACE. Later work optimizes protected-statement counts and statistically profiles acquisition/contention~\cite{cernyquality}; preemption-safety synthesis also addresses deadlock-free lock placement~\cite{cernysafe}. Finite $n,B,r$ instances admit general-game encodings; our archived initial-total-cost potential proves neither source equivalence nor intermediate-state quotients.

Mannor et al.~\cite{mannor} supply global-deviation-budget DP; Postek et al.~\cite{postek} give finite-candidate DP and causal budgeted routing. Information-state minimax control~\cite{dave} also precedes our recurrence. Our specialization proves that independently realizable intervals and monotonicity reduce repair feasibility to a minimum-consistent-write path; backward induction itself is inherited.

\paragraph{Online algorithms with predictions.}
Karlin et al.~\cite{karlin1988}, Section 4/Theorem 4.1, use cost-balancing counters for competitive bus communication against an offline comparator. Our call-capped additive loss and causal budget-informed comparator differ.
Purohit, Svitkina and Kumar~\cite{purohit2018}, Algorithm 2/Theorem 2.2, trade consistency against robustness using predicted ski days and a trust parameter. Lykouris and Vassilvitskii~\cite{lykouris2018}, Sections 2--3, bound expected cache misses against an offline optimum across next-request prediction errors. Wei and Zhang~\cite{wei2020}, Theorems 1.1--1.2, give tight ski-rental tradeoffs. Our comparator knows only the write bound and remains causal. We characterize one-write saturation for a no-advice policy equaling this informed minimax curve at every budget under a universal cap.

\paragraph{Incremental computation and repair costs.}
Mokhov, Mitchell and Peyton Jones~\cite{mokhov2018}, Definitions 2.1/3.1 and Sections 4--6, classify build schedulers/rebuilders using persistent dependencies; Section 6.6 bounds iterative execution. Ley-Wild, Acar and Fluet~\cite{leywild2009}, Theorems 4--5, relate consistent change propagation to trace-distance cost. Adapton~\cite{adapton2014}, Theorem 4.3/Algorithm 1, ensures nonincremental-result consistency and demand-driven propagation; dirty-edge value checks can avoid recomputation. These guarantees differ from our fixed footprint-union cost and the choice to accept damage or spend a call on fresh preparation. They do not state our acceptance value or common-policy boundary.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limits}

The whole-kernel contract excludes replenishment, structural change, exceptions, external completion and retained guards. Retained charged guards improve five of 384 inputs (e.g., 242 versus 243), and one intermediate state improves from 45 to 43. Equality for the stronger interface remains unproved.

Selected Roslyn paths and shared-compiler checks do not establish arbitrary concurrent equivalence. Our patch deadlocks/timeouts distinguish replayability from safe protected execution. Rebase costs remain uncalibrated; general footprint profiling may be exponential and native validation may reveal different information.

One-workstation authored arrivals, instrumentation and cache/JIT effects limit timing inference; human benefit is unmeasured. Archived scalar gains occur in 2/96 cells, timing fits succeed in 2/8, and all-budget compilation can exceed requested-value costs. Finite tests and AI-assisted inspection are neither mechanical proofs nor independent reproductions.

\section{Conclusion}

Cached completion attains the specified interface's whole-kernel frontier under one universal call cap, at possible total-work and foreground-time costs. Partial repair and call charges change the information boundary; finite synthesis also minimizes uniform additive loss when no common optimum exists. Source checks expose preservation obligations and count tradeoffs; timing benefits depend on costs and contention.
